### City context & character

Toronto is, in an important sense, a city that resisted being a city for a long time. It was founded in 1793 as "York" by Lieutenant Governor John Graves Simcoe, who laid out a grid settlement on the north shore of Lake Ontario partly to anchor Upper Canada against American encroachment. Simcoe imagined a Loyalist colonial capital — hierarchical, Church of England, deliberately un-American — and the temperament stuck even after York was renamed Toronto and incorporated as a city in 1834. The Americans did burn it in 1813 during the War of 1812 (they called it a raid; the British called it an atrocity; Canadians use it to explain why British troops later burned Washington), leaving little of the original colonial fabric. What you see in the downtown core is mostly post-1850, much of it post-April 1904, when the Great Fire destroyed over 120 buildings in the commercial warehouse district south of King Street in two days, gutting the blocks between Bay and York Streets down to the waterfront.

The late 19th and early 20th centuries gave Toronto its institutional architecture: University College from 1859 (one of the oldest buildings on the U of T campus), the old City Hall designed by E.J. Lennox and completed 1899, the ROM which opened in 1912. The city's self-image during this period was resolutely British-Canadian, Protestant, and smug about it — "Toronto the Good" was the nickname, usually ironic, reflecting strict liquor control, Sunday commerce bans, and a civic culture that found jazz clubs faintly disreputable. The postwar decades remade it faster than almost any other North American city. Immigration from Italy, Portugal, the Caribbean, Hong Kong, South Asia, and Eastern Europe (including a substantial wave of Polish displaced persons after 1945, and later Solidarity-era emigrants after 1981) broke the Anglo-Protestant monoculture and built the neighbourhood textures that make Toronto interesting today. Kensington Market shifted from Jewish to Portuguese-Caribbean-Vietnamese across four decades. Little Italy on College Street is now more Korean than Italian. Roncesvalles remains recognizably Polish. The municipal amalgamation of 1998 — when Premier Mike Harris merged six independent municipalities (Toronto, North York, Scarborough, Etobicoke, York, East York) into a single city of 2.6 million over the objections of most residents — remains one of the most resented political decisions in the city's modern history. Many of today's planning and civic debates trace back to what was lost in that merger.

Two contemporary controversies are worth knowing before you walk around. First: Toronto Metropolitan University (formerly Ryerson University) renamed itself in 2022 after the statue of Egerton Ryerson — the 19th-century educator who architected Canada's residential school system, which forcibly removed Indigenous children from their families — was pulled down by protesters in June 2021, shortly after the Tk'emlúps te Secwépemc announced the discovery of unmarked graves at a former school in Kamloops. The renaming debate exposed a genuine fault line in how the city reckons with its colonial foundations. Second: the city voted in 2021 to rename streets honouring Henry Dundas, 1st Viscount Melville, who delayed the abolition of the slave trade in the British Parliament by approximately fifteen years. Dundas Street is Toronto's great east-west artery; the renaming and its implementation have been slow and contested. [TODO: verify current status of Dundas Street renaming as of mid-2026]

### Things to do — math-adjacent

**1. The Fields Institute, 222 College Street**
You will be here for the conference, but take a moment to understand what the building represents. The Institute was founded in 1992 and is named for John Charles Fields (1863–1932), the University of Toronto mathematician who established the International Medal for Outstanding Discoveries in Mathematics — what is now called the Fields Medal. Fields taught at U of T from 1902 until his death and used his own estate to fund the medal, dying before he could see it awarded. The Institute's lobby and common areas typically display mathematical art, models, and historical material about Fields and the medal's history. The library is accessible to conference participants and holds a substantial working research collection.

**2. Thomas Fisher Rare Book Library, 120 St. George Street**
A ten-minute walk from the venue. One of the significant research libraries in Canada, holding a first edition of Newton's *Principia Mathematica* (1687), Galileo's *Sidereus Nuncius* (1610), and substantial holdings in the history of mathematics and natural philosophy. [TODO: verify the specific items currently accessible for viewing in 2026 — some items are held in closed stacks and require prior arrangement.] Admission to the reading room is free; the small exhibition cases along the entrance corridor are often overlooked by visitors and frequently display items from the mathematics and scientific instrument collections. If you want to see a specific item from the main collection, contact the library in advance.

**3. J.C. Fields' Grave, Mount Pleasant Cemetery, 750 Mount Pleasant Road**
Mount Pleasant is Toronto's great Victorian garden cemetery, opened in 1876 on a plan modeled on the American rural cemetery movement — a deliberate contrast to the crowded urban churchyards it was meant to replace. Fields is buried here; the grave is modest relative to his institutional legacy. [TODO: verify section/lot — the cemetery office at the main gate maintains a searchable database and can direct you to the precise location.] The cemetery is also the resting place of Glenn Gould and William Lyon Mackenzie King, among others from the city's scientific and cultural establishment. About 4 km north of the venue; take the 97 Yonge bus from Bloor station or walk through Rosedale.

**4. Robarts Library and University of Toronto St. George Campus**
The main U of T research library (opened 1973, architects Warner Burns Toan & Lunde) is one of the more genuinely strange brutalist buildings in North America — a massive concrete structure informally called "Fort Book," or less affectionately nicknamed after a turkey (the floor plan from above loosely resembles one). The stacks are open access and the mathematics collection is substantial. Walk south through campus after visiting: University College (1859, Romanesque Revival by Cumberland & Storm) is the architectural anchor. The stone carvings on the south facade include standard academic motifs and others that appear to have been improvised by the stonemasons. Look for the bullet hole in the east wall of the original wing — it dates from an 1890 shooting incident involving a graduate student, which became a minor national scandal at the time.

**5. Department of Mathematics, 40 St. George Street**
The mathematics building is a mid-century concrete slab, architecturally uninteresting, but the departmental corridors hold photographs, documents, and archival material related to J.C. Fields and the early history of Canadian mathematics. If you ask at the front desk, staff can typically point you to what's on display. **6. Hart House, 7 Hart House Circle**
Built between 1911 and 1919 in the Collegiate Gothic style and funded by Vincent Massey (later the first Canadian-born Governor General), Hart House holds a permanent art collection distributed throughout the building — not in a conventional gallery space, but in stairwells, corridors, and common rooms. The collection includes significant work by the Group of Seven alongside historical portraits. Entry is free for those on campus; the Great Hall has hammer-beam timber construction comparable to the best English collegiate examples. Less obviously: Hart House has hosted mathematical seminars and lectures throughout U of T's history, and the library within the building has a collection reflecting the institution's academic patronage.

### Things to do — culture, food, walks

**Kensington Market**
Not a farmers' market in the conventional sense. Kensington is a small grid of streets — Baldwin, Augusta, Kensington Avenue itself — that have operated as a market district since the early 20th century, first as a Jewish market when the surrounding blocks housed the Eastern European Jewish community that arrived after 1900, then shifting to Portuguese and Caribbean after the postwar immigration waves, then accumulating further layers. The buildings are mostly two- and three-storey Victorian rowhouses converted to retail; the street-level businesses turn over constantly but the physical fabric does not change. Walk north on Augusta on a weekday morning to get the actual market character rather than the evening and weekend bar scene. The area is under sustained development pressure; the density and affordability that characterize it now are not guaranteed indefinitely. This is a 10-minute walk southeast from the Fields Institute.

**Roncesvalles Avenue**
Take the 506 Carlton streetcar west to Roncesvalles Avenue, then walk north. This is the historical centre of Polish Toronto — a community shaped by specific historical pressures that are legible in the neighbourhood's social institutions if you know what to look for. The postwar wave (1945–1952) was predominantly displaced persons and veterans of the Polish Armed Forces in the West who could not safely return to the Soviet-controlled Polish People's Republic; the Solidarity wave after 1981 added a second layer of political emigrants. The parish church on the avenue [TODO: verify exact church name — the main Polish Catholic parish is likely Our Lady of Czestochowa or St. Casimir's] still holds masses in Polish. There are Polish delis and bakeries along the stretch between Queen Street West and Howard Park Avenue. The Polish Combatants' Association Hall [TODO: verify current status and address] reflects the specific character of the veterans' community that settled here after 1945. For someone holding a Polish passport and presumably familiar with that history, Roncesvalles offers something more substantive than the tourist Polish quarters of other cities — a working-class diasporic settlement built by people who lost the war they won.

**The Distillery District**
A 2.5 km walk southeast from the Fields Institute, or 10 minutes on the 504 King streetcar east. The Victorian industrial complex of Gooderham & Worts Distillery (founded 1832, by the 1860s the largest distillery in the British Empire) was preserved intact when production ended and converted into a pedestrianized arts district in the early 2000s. The brick buildings, cobblestone lanes, and industrial infrastructure — cast-iron columns, vaulted basement cellars, barrel-aging warehouses — are largely original. It is now somewhat overpriced and tourist-oriented, particularly on weekends, but architecturally it is the best-preserved Victorian industrial campus in Toronto. Come on a weekday morning to see it without crowds. The Soulpepper Theatre Company is based here, and the contemporary art galleries along the eastern end are uneven but occasionally worth checking.

**St. Lawrence Market and the Old Town**
South of King Street, the St. Lawrence neighbourhood contains more pre-1904 fire fabric than anywhere else in the downtown core. The St. Lawrence Market (92 Front Street East) has been a public market on this site since 1803; the current South Market building dates to 1844 with an 1898 extension incorporating the original Toronto City Hall facade — a strange piece of architectural palimpsest where the government building is literally embedded inside the market building. The Saturday farmers' market in the North Market building is the one worth going to. Walk the surrounding streets: the blocks between Front, King, Jarvis, and Yonge preserve a scale and commercial character that the area west of Bay Street lost to the 1904 fire and subsequent redevelopment. The St. James Cathedral (65 Church Street) was substantially rebuilt after the fire of 1849 and again after the 1904 fire; the current building's tower was completed in 1874 and is one of the few genuinely old pieces of vertical fabric in the city.

**Café Diplomatico, 594 College Street**
An institution on College Street since 1968, when the street was the spine of Italian Toronto. The neighbourhood has shifted substantially — more Korean and South Asian now — but Diplomatico maintained its outdoor patio culture and its particular clientele. The food is unremarkable; what matters is that it is one of the few spots in the immediate conference area where sitting outside for two hours over a $4 espresso is normal and unhurried. Useful for post-session decompression, and the foot traffic on College Street at any hour gives a reasonable cross-section of the neighbourhood's current character.

**Bloor Street West, the Annex, and Bloor Hot Docs Cinema**
The Annex was annexed into Toronto from the village of Yorkville in 1887; the housing stock is late Victorian and Edwardian rowhouses (roughly 1885–1910), many rented by U of T graduate students and faculty. Walk north from the Fields Institute on Spadina to Bloor, then east toward Avenue Road. The Bloor Hot Docs Cinema (506 Bloor Street West) is a restored 1938 cinema that runs the documentary film circuit; it is architecturally one of the better small-scale theatre buildings in the city, and checking what's on during conference week is worth a few minutes. Several independent bookshops with decent academic and used sections remain on this stretch; the density of bookshops has declined since the 2000s but not disappeared.

**Trinity Bellwoods Park and Museum of Contemporary Art Toronto (MOCA)**
Trinity Bellwoods (20 minutes southwest of the Fields Institute on foot, or take the 501 Queen streetcar west to Strachan) occupies the grounds of a former Anglican college; the original 1851 gateposts are still at the Queen Street entrance and are the only remaining structural element of the original Trinity College building. The park is large enough to have a genuine interior, away from the street noise. Further west along Queen Street to Ossington and then north on Dufferin to Sterling Road is the Museum of Contemporary Art Toronto (MOCA, 158 Sterling Road), housed since 2018 in a converted industrial building in the Junction Triangle. MOCA has been artistically interesting and financially precarious since it opened; worth checking whether there's a current exhibition before making the trip.

### Beyond the city — day trips & nearby regions

**Niagara-on-the-Lake and Niagara Falls** (1.5–2 hours by car; GO Transit + Niagara bus service available, though schedules can be infrequent — [TODO: verify current GO/regional transit connections to Niagara region])
The more interesting day centres on Niagara-on-the-Lake rather than the falls themselves. This was Newark, the first capital of Upper Canada, burned more comprehensively than York was by American forces in December 1813 and rebuilt in Georgian style through the 1820s — giving it an unusual architectural coherence for Ontario. Fort George, the British garrison on the river, is reconstructed on its original footprint and provides a clearer sense of the War of 1812 geography than anything in Toronto. The Niagara Falls themselves are genuinely impressive in the way that geological scale is impressive: the Horseshoe Falls are 57 metres, the American Falls 21–34 metres, and both have been significantly modified by hydroelectric diversions since the early 20th century — the flow you see today is a regulated fraction of the original. The tourist infrastructure on the Ontario side is aggressively bad; arrive before 9am, walk to Table Rock, and leave before the crowds thicken.

**Kingston, Ontario** (2.5 hours by Via Rail; trains run multiple times daily, approximately $30–70 CAD return depending on advance booking)
Kingston is the most historically legible city in Ontario. Founded in 1673 as Fort Frontenac (a French military post and trading base on Haudenosaunee territory), destroyed by the British in 1758, rebuilt as a Loyalist garrison town, briefly the capital of the Province of Canada from 1841 to 1844 before the capital was moved to Montreal and eventually Ottawa. The limestone architecture — quarried locally from the Frontenac Arch formation — gives the city a visual consistency that Ontario towns built in brick or wood lack. Key sites: City Hall (1843, one of the best neoclassical civic buildings in the country), Fort Henry on the hill east of downtown (a War of 1812-era fortification maintained as a heritage site), and the Kingston Penitentiary (opened 1835, closed 2013, now offering public tours — worth booking in advance). Queen's University's Gothic Revival campus was founded in 1841 and has a mathematics department with historical depth. The Rideau Canal, which departs from Kingston Mills just north of the city and runs 202 km north to Ottawa, was built in 1826–1832 as a military bypass to protect against American blockade of the St. Lawrence; it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and still fully operational.

**Ottawa** (4.5 hours by Via Rail; approximately $60–100 CAD return with advance booking — the overnight or weekend trip is better value than the pure day trip)
Ottawa became capital of the Province of Canada in 1857 — chosen by Queen Victoria in principle, though the actual decision was made by the colonial government partly to sidestep the Montreal-Toronto rivalry. Parliament Hill is High Victorian Gothic Revival; the original Centre Block burned in 1916 and was replaced with the current building, completed in 1927. The Peace Tower was added later still. The National Gallery of Canada (Moshe Safdie, 1988) has the best collection of Canadian art in the country and a solid European wing; the glass towers overlooking the Ottawa River are intentionally cathedral-like in reference to the nearby Notre-Dame Basilica. The Canadian War Museum (2005) is architecturally deliberate in a way that most war museums are not — the main gallery roof is oriented to receive the dawn light on November 11. The Library and Archives Canada (395 Wellington Street) holds the national documentary heritage collection and is accessible to researchers; for anyone interested in the history of Canadian science or mathematics, this is the primary archival source.

**Hamilton** (55 minutes by GO Transit train from Union Station; trains run frequently, approximately $12–15 CAD one way)
Hamilton is the most interesting Ontario city that Toronto tends to dismiss. It was the industrial core of English Canada through most of the 20th century — steel mills, manufacturing, union politics — and the deindustrialization of the 1970s and 1980s left a working-class city with a significant arts community filling the abandoned industrial fabric. James Street North is the main arts corridor, with independent galleries, the Workers' Arts and Heritage Centre (51 Stuart Street, in a former Customs and Excise building from 1860 — one of the better adaptive reuse projects in the province), and the Supercrawl festival in September. From a historical standpoint, Hamilton was a significant node on the Underground Railroad — the Freedom Road corridor through southern Ontario brought freedom seekers through Hamilton to the nearby village of Ancaster and further north, and the Black community history here predates Confederation. The Art Gallery of Hamilton (123 King Street West) holds substantial Canadian modernist work and is chronically undervisited. Architecturally, the downtown retains intact 1890s–1920s commercial streetscapes that were demolished in Toronto during the postwar urban renewal campaigns.
