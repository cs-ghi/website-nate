\begin{document}
\maketitle

Banach spaces admit a notion of size per elements. We can estimate for how
this notion of size changes when adding or multiplying them (triangle inequality, Minkowski inequality, etc.). A key
feature that is not present in these spaces is the granular information about how much two elements are similar. For
example, how much is a vector a \emph{linear} combination of a single series of functions with different
scalar weights, rather than just the convergence of a series where we cannot isolate the ``contribution'' of
one element over another. If we had this information, we could project onto basis elements and meaningfully
compare elements. In the same vein, is there a way to measure the ``defect'' in the triangle
inequality? That is, can we find a reliable functional $J(f,g)$ such that:
\[
	\|f+g\|_p^2 = \|f\|_p^2 + \|g\|_p^2 + J(f,g)
\]
Such niceties are in fact not found in general Banach spaces due to algebraic blockers (or, interpreted
geometrically, due to the non-flat geometry of most Banach spaces).

To see this, let us consider how to connect an element $f \in L^p$ to its natural functional (we shall justify
natural shortly). First, what does it mean to go in the ``same direction'' in a function space? (For
simplicity we'll always work with $L^p$ spaces\footnote{the reasoning can extend to Fr\'echet by taking limit
  of Hilbert spaces, but a new approach is needed for metric spaces, called CAT(k) geometry,
see~\cite{millerGEOMETRYMEASURECATk} for more details}). If $f,g \in L^p$, then we would say that $f+g$
implies moving in the direction of $g$ along the ``same line''; this matches our finite-dimensional intuition.
Then $f+f = 2f$ implies moving in the same direction as $f$. Let us say that we want a functional that
represents going in the direction of $f$, namely $\phi_f \in (L^p)^*$ such that $\phi_f(f) = \|f\|_p$. This is
a natural choice: in finite dimensions we interpret functionals as measuring how much a vector projects onto a
direction. As $(L^p)^* \cong L^q$ , it is easy to see that this functional corresponds to $g \in L^q$ such
that:
\[
	g(x) = |f(x)|^{p-1}\sgn(f(x))
\]
Note that $g \in L^q$ since:
\[
	|g|^q = |f|^{q(p-1)} = |f|^{qp - q} \overset{!}{=}|f|^p
\]
where $\overset{!}{=}$ comes since $1/p + 1/q = 1$ iff $p+q = pq$ iff $p = q(p-1)$. Hence as $f \in L^p$
\[
	\int |g|^q = \int |f|^p < \infty
\]
Thus, we have a map $f \mapsto \phi_f$. Let us define the duality map:
\[
	J_p: L^p \to L^q \qquad f \mapsto \phi_f
\]
This map is \emph{not} linear:
\[
	J_p(2f) = |2f|^{p-1}\sgn(2f) = 2^{p-1}|f|^{p-1}\sgn(f) = 2^{p-1}J_p(f)
\]
As $(L^q)^* \cong L^p$, we can interpret this as a map $J_p: L^p \to (L^p)^*$.
This is only linear in the special case of $p =2$, since:
\[
	J_2(nf) = |nf|^{2-1}\sgn(nf) = n J_2(f)
\]
Why is this specific functional the ``natural'' choice? In functional analysis, the Hahn-Banach theorem
guarantees that for any non-zero element $f$, there exists at least one functional $\phi$ of unit norm such
that $\phi(f) = \|f\|$. Geometrically, the kernel of this functional defines a closed hyperplane that touches
(supports) the ball of radius $\|f\|$ exactly at the point $f$.


In rougher spaces like $L^1$ or $L^\infty$, the unit balls have sharp corners and flat edges. At a corner, you
can balance infinitely many different flat sheets (hyperplanes) on the tip; essentially, the "direction" of
the functional is ambiguous. However, for $1 < p < \infty$, the $L^p$ spaces are smooth and strictly convex.
Note that a norm is smooth if the G\^ateau derivative for any two $x,y$ away form the origin exists:
\[
\lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\|x + ty\| - \|x\|}{t} \quad \text{exists.}
\]

For $L^p$ where $1 < p < \infty$, the unit ball is smooth. Consequently, at every point $f$ on
the surface, there is exactly one unique tangent plane. The duality map $J_p(f)$ we derived above is simply
the unique algebraic description of this tangent plane. It is the only functional that aligns perfectly with
the geometry of the surface at that specific point.


This can be thought of geometrically: when $p \ne 2$, as we move further from the origin, the `strength'
or sensitivity of the geometry grows non-linearly. This effectively warps the space so that large vectors
interact differently than small ones (in terms of physics intuition, the ``cost'' of moving away from the
origin depends on how far out you are).

Let us connect this using actual geometry. Consider the function that defines the geometry of the $L^p$ unit ball:
\[
F(x) = \frac{1}{p} \|x\|_p^p = \frac{1}{p} \sum |x_i|^p
\]
(where the $\ell^p$ norm is used for visualization, but the logic holds for integrals).
From multivariable calculus, we know the unit sphere is the Level Set $F(x) = \frac{1}{p}$, and the gradient
$\nabla F(x)$ is the vector strictly perpendicular (normal) to the level set at point $x$.
Calculating the gradient:
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \left( \frac{1}{p} |x_i|^p \right) = |x_i|^{p-1} \text{sgn}(x_i)
\]
So, the gradient vector is:
\[
\nabla F(x) = \left( |x_1|^{p-1}\text{sgn}(x_1), \dots, |x_n|^{p-1}\text{sgn}(x_n) \right)
\]
Notice that this is exactly our duality map:
\[
\nabla F(x) = J_p(x)
\]
Thus, $J_p(x)$ is the right candidate to represent the geometry of $L^p$\footnote{If $p = 1$,
notice that $J_1(x) = \sgn(x)$ has infinitely many possibilities at zero, mirroring the fact that the $L^1$ unit ball
is a jagged diamond with corners having infinitely many tangents.}.

Let us now understand how this relates the inner product, in particular consider how we recover the
inner product in Euclidean space. If we define the energy of a vector as $E(x) = \frac{1}{2}\|x\|_2^2$, then
the directional derivative of this energy at $x$ in the direction of a perturbation $y$ is:
\[
	\frac{d}{dt} E(x + ty) \Big|_{t=0} = \frac{d}{dt} \frac{1}{2} \langle x+ty, x+ty \rangle \Big|_{t=0} = \langle x, y \rangle
\]
Thus, the inner product $\langle x, y \rangle$ is not just an algebraic artifact; it physically represents
the \emph{sensitivity} of the norm of $x$ to a perturbation by $y$.

In $L^p$ spaces, we lack a global inner product, but we can still ask this fundamental question: how does the
$p$-energy $F(f) = \frac{1}{p}\|f\|_p^p$ change if we perturb $f$ by a function $g$? Using the gradient we
just calculated, the first-order variation is\footnote{The $dF$ here can be thought of as the G\^ateaux
derivative, commonly defined for functions between locally convex topological vector spaces}:
\[
d F(f; g) = \langle \nabla F(f), g \rangle_{\text{dual}} = \int \nabla F(f) \cdot g
\]
Substituting $\nabla F = J_p(f)$, this variation becomes exactly $\int J_p(f)g$. Thus, we can define a pairing:
\[
	\langle f,g \rangle_p = \int J_p(f)g
\]
Note that it is important to go through $J_p(f)$ since $\int fg$ need not be finite in general. However, by H\"older's Inequality:
\[
	\int |J_p(f)g| < \infty
\]
As noted, this is \emph{not} an inner product, primarily because it is not symmetric! This hints that the geometry
of $L^p$ is \emph{not} flat for $p \ne 2$. If however $p = 2$, then $J_p(x)$ maps identically to itself and we
recover the symmetric form $\int fg$.

This lack of symmetry in the pairing $\langle f,g \rangle_p$ is directly linked to the fact that $L^2$ is the
unique $L^p$ space that is \emph{rotationally invariant}. Geometrically, rotational invariance means that the
properties of a vector depend only on its length, not on its alignment with the coordinate axes.
Mathematically, this requires that the duality map commutes with rotations. If $R$ is a rotation operator
(an isometry), we expect the ``normal vector'' of the rotated point to be the rotated ``normal
vector'' of the original point:
\[
	J_p(Rf) \overset{?}{=} R(J_p(f))
\]
In $L^2$, since $J_2$ is linear (essentially the identity), this holds trivially: $J_2(Rf) = Rf = R(J_2(f))$.
However, for $p \ne 2$, the map $f \mapsto |f|^{p-1}\sgn(f)$ applies a non-linear distortion component-wise.
Rotating the vector mixes the components, and applying the power law \emph{after} mixing is not the same as
applying it \emph{before}.

To see this concretely, consider $\ell^3$ ($p=3$) in $\R^2$ and let $f = (1, 0)$. If we first compute the functional and then rotate by $\pi/4$ ($45^\circ$), we get:
\[
	R(J_3(f)) = R(1^2, 0^2) = R(1, 0) = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
\]
However, if we rotate the vector first, the components become $(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})$.
Applying the duality map $J_3$ (squaring components) yields:
\[
	J_3(Rf) = J_3\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right) = \left( \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2} \right)
\]
Since $(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}) \ne (\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$, we have $J_p(Rf) \ne
R(J_p(f))$. This confirms that for $p \ne 2$, the geometry is \emph{anisotropic}: the shape of the unit ball looks different depending on the angle from which you view it.

This distinction can be understood most rigorously through the lens of Riemannian geometry. While the pairing
$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_p$ corresponds to the first derivative (gradient) of the energy, the
geometry of the space is determined by how this gradient \emph{changes}---the second derivative,
which acts as the metric tensor. In this framework, the metric tensor $g_{ij}(x)$ at a point $x$ dictates how
to measure the length of tangent vectors. In our context, this metric is generated by the Hessian of the
energy function $E(x) = \frac{1}{p}\|x\|_p^p$. For $L^2$, this Hessian is proportional to the identity
operator everywhere; the metric is \emph{constant} (flat). However, for $L^p$ ($p \ne 2$), the second
derivative depends on position $x$, specifically scaling as $|x|^{p-2}$. This implies that the ``metric''
changes as we move around the space.

To visualize the practical consequences of this non-flat geometry, it is helpful to consider the ``shape'' of
the unit ball in infinite dimensions. In finite dimensions, all norms are equivalent, meaning the unit ball of
one norm can always be fit inside a scaled unit ball of another. For example, in $\R^n$, the $\ell^1$ diamond
fits inside the $\ell^\infty$ cube, and the cube fits inside a scaled diamond. The ratio of their sizes is
bounded by the dimension $n$. This means that a ``spike'' in the $\ell^1$ ball has a finite maximum length
relative to the $\ell^\infty$ norm; you cannot travel infinitely far down a spike without eventually hitting
the origin in every other norm. Topologically, this guarantees that convergence in one norm implies
convergence in all others.\footnote{in particular, they define the same \emph{topology} even if the norms
define different \emph{geometries}}

In infinite dimensions, this equivalence breaks because the dimension $n$ --- and thus the scaling factor
between norms --- goes to infinity. The curvature of the norm now dictates how ``energy'' is allowed to
concentrate without bound. The $L^\infty$ unit ball acts like a strict box: it bounds the height of a function
everywhere, preventing energy concentration but allowing mass to spread out infinitely. Conversely, the $L^1$
unit ball allows for infinitely long thin spikes: it allows functions to become arbitrarily tall as long as
they are thin enough (concentration of measure), effectively extending ``spikes'' out to infinity relative to
the $L^\infty$ norm.

This geometric disparity explains why convergence depends on the norm. Convergence $f_n \to 0$ is not about
movement, but about entering arbitrarily small $\epsilon$-balls. Consider the sequence $f_n(x) = x^n$ on
$[0,1]$. In the $L^1$ geometry, this sequence travels down one of the ``spikes'' of the unit ball: the functions get
thinner and thinner, their area (and thus distance to the origin) shrinking to zero despite their height
remaining constant. In the $L^\infty$ geometry, however, these functions are trapped on the surface of the unit
sphere (height 1); they cannot enter the $\epsilon$-ball because the $L^\infty$ ``box'' does not extend down
the ``spikes'' of concentration.

The Hilbert space $L^2$ sits perfectly in the middle. Its unit ball is a round sphere, balancing height and
width. If $L^1$ can be thought of as having no penalties to concentrate energy, and $L^\infty$ has no penalty
in spreading mass, $L^2$ creates a geometry that penalizes both extreme concentration (spikes) and extreme
spread, providing the only setting where energy behaves isotropically (again, visualized by the fact the unit
ball has rotational invariance). Thus, while $L^2$ is a flat Euclidean plane, general Banach spaces behave
like curved manifolds where the very definition of angle and length is position-dependent. For further
geometric discussion of Banach spaces, see \emph{Geometry of Banach Spaces} by Diestel
\cite{diestelGeometryBanachSpacesSelected1975}.

With this understanding of the position-dependent metric, we can clarify the notion of orthogonality.
In Euclidean geometry, orthogonality is symmetric ($x \perp y \iff y \perp x$) and additive. In
general Banach spaces, without a global bilinear form, we must rely on \emph{Birkhoff-James orthogonality}: we say $x \perp y$ if moving in the direction of $y$ does not decrease the length of $x$ ($\|x + \lambda y\| \ge \|x\|$). Geometrically, this means the line passing through $x$ in the direction of $y$ is tangent to the unit sphere at $x$.

Analytically, utilizing the functional $J_p$, this condition is equivalent to requiring that $y$ be annihilated by the functional generated by $x$:
\[
	\langle J_p(x), y \rangle = 0
\]
This reveals the fundamental flaw in non-Hilbert geometry. In $L^2$, because $J_2$ is linear, the condition is symmetric. However, in $L^p$ ($p \ne 2$), the non-linearity of $J_p$ breaks reciprocity. The set of vectors orthogonal to $x$ (kernel of $J_p(x)$) does not align symmetrically with the set of vectors orthogonal to $y$. Consequently, it is common to have $x \perp y$ but $y \not\perp x$. This lack of symmetry illustrates why Hilbert spaces are the unique setting where Euclidean geometric intuition remains valid.

The unique geometry of Hilbert spaces---specifically self-duality, isotropy, and the inner product---unlocks
powerful results unavailable in general Banach spaces. The following are some key consequences:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{The Projection Theorem (Best Approximation):}
    In general Banach spaces, finding the element in a subspace $M$ closest to a point $x$ is difficult (the
    closest point might not be unique, e.g., the flat edge of $L^1$). In Hilbert spaces, the strict convexity and ``roundness'' of the unit ball guarantee that for any closed subspace $M$, there exists a \emph{unique} element $P_M x$ minimizing the distance $\|x - m\|$. Furthermore, the error vector $x - P_M x$ is orthogonal to the subspace.

    \item \emph{The Riesz Representation Theorem (Self-Duality):}
    The duality map $J: H \to H^*$ is linear and bijective. This means we can completely identify linear functionals with vectors. Every continuous linear functional $\phi$ acts exactly like an inner product: $\phi(x) = \langle x, y \rangle$ for a unique $y$. This collapses the study of $H^*$ to the study of $H$.

	\item \emph{Orthonormal Bases and Fourier Series:} In $L^p$, we may have a Schauder basis\footnote{A sequence $\{b_n\}$ where every $v \in V$ has a unique expansion $v = \sum \alpha_nb_n$}, but determining the coefficients is non-trivial. In Hilbert spaces, orthogonality allows us to decouple coefficients. This is key for the Fourier transform, where any element is reconstructed by projection: $x = \sum \langle x, e_n \rangle e_n$. Additionally, the \emph{Parseval Identity} allows us to measure size by summing squared coefficients: $\|x\|^2 = \sum |\langle x, e_n \rangle|^2$.

    \item \emph{Existence of Adjoints:}
    In Banach spaces, the adjoint of $T: X \to Y$ maps $Y^* \to X^*$. Because Hilbert spaces are self-dual, we can pull this operator back to the original space. Thus, for every bounded operator $T$ on a Hilbert space, there is a unique adjoint $T^*$ on the \emph{same space} satisfying $\langle Tx, y \rangle = \langle x, T^* y \rangle$. This is foundational for spectral theory.
\end{enumerate}

In summary, while Banach spaces provide the necessary topological framework (completeness), Hilbert spaces add the rigid geometric structure required to generalize linear algebra to infinite dimensions.
