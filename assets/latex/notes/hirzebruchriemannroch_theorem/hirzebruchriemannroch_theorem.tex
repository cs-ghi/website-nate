\textcolor{red}{don't forget to get rid of all the "We"s}

In a sense, the introduction will pretend to be accessible, but we shall inevitably have to
assume some background in a little bit of everything in geometry and algebra: Riemann geometry, complex and
algebraic geometry, Basic K-theory and the theory of characteristic classes, and Elementary Hodge theory.
The introduction can be thought of as me trying to make the ideas of the paper as accessible as possible to
an undergraduate, but that goal has to drop as soon as we start proving things or else it will the paper
will be hundreds of pages of background material.

Also, I chose to define the Chern character in a ``classical'' way as given by
Hatcher~\cite{hatcherVectorBundlesKTheory2017} or Hirzebruch~\cite{hirzebruchTopologicalMethodsAlgebraic1995}
instead of the ``intersection theory'' interpretation (for ex. given by~\cite{fultonIntersectionTheory1984}),
however I will point out some interesting ways in which the two are linked (ex. A quick connection to Serge
classes).

I also do a fundamentally different thing from Hirzebruch: besides being the literal opposite of
``Bourbaki-dry'' I also change the order to present the geometric material and $\chi_y$-characteristic
\emph{first} as it totally motivates why the Todd class is defined in this context. Hirzebruch does the
opposite because he has been infected with the Bourbaki-dryness that I want to avoid.

This paper will try to contain as many proofs as possible, but it is best considered as a foray of the path
that build up to Hirzebruch Riemann Roch.

Ultimate Hirzebruch goal:
\[
  T_1 = \chi_1 = \tau
\]
which is enough for them to agree.

Notation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item I would like that $K$ or $k$ represent a field, but $K$ is already being used for $K(-)$ or the
    $K$-genus while $k$ is often used for indexing and so it is too much, so let $\F$ represent an arbitrary
    field.
  \item Let if $H_i^* \times H^{n-i}$ is a perfect pairing, as the manifolds/varieties we are working with are
    nice enough we shall abuse notation due to DeRham's Theorem and write:
    \[
      \int_Y c
    \]
    for the pairing of the cohomology class $c$ with $Y$. If we want to make it more explicit, we shall write
    $\kappa_n[-]$ notation.
   \item $A\dcup B$ represents disjoint union
   \item $TM$ and $T_M$ represent tangent bundles. $N_{M/N}$ represent the normal bundle of $N$ with respect
     to $M$.
\end{enumerate}

Background: the minimum is (where any author can be replaced with an equivalent):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item algebra up to D\&F
  \item Hartshorne
  \item complex analysis up to Alford
  \item differential geometry up to John M. Lee
  \item algebraic topology up to Hatcher
\end{enumerate}

I will introduce the necessary Hodge Theory and cobordism theory, and only the characteristic theory that is
necessary.

Particularities:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Riemann geometry: Riemann metric, Levi-Civita connection, Gauss-Bonnet, signature, orientation.
    See~\cite{NateDiffGeo}
  \item cohomology theory: Hatcher level of knowledge (\cite{hatcherAlgebraicTopology}), or
    see~\cite{NateAlgTop}.
   \item Theory of symmetric function (the basis that can be found in many group theory/field theory textbooks):
     see~\cite{NateAlgebra}.
    \item Hartshorne level of algebraic geometry
    \item Alford level of complex analysis.
\end{enumerate}

Bonus section in chapter~\ref{cha:RR} about Grothendeick Riemann Roch which will be self-contained and will be
to show what is different in the generalization and the key steps for the generalization.

\chapter{Motivation: Riemann Roch Problem}


A natural question to ask in complex and algebraic geometry is ``how many
holomorphic/meromorphic/algebraic/analytic/etc functions are there on a (suitable) space $X$''. By letting
$\CF(X)$ represent our choice of functions (the global sections of a [quasi-coherent] sheaf $\CF$), we shall
denote this question as finding information about the $0$th sheaf cohomological group
\[
  H^0(X, \CF)
\]
which by~\cite{NateAlgGeo} is equal to $\CF(X)$. We denote $\CF(X)$ in terms of sheaf cohomology because this
group shall sometimes be trivial, and the reason for its trivialness can be described by higher order sheaf
cohomology groups:
\[
  H^1(X, \CF), H^2(X, \CF), ...
\]
exactly how to extract this information is known as the \emph{Riemann Roch Problem}. To motivate the direction
the problem took, let us answer this question in some simple cases, build up to how the Riemann Roch question
is answer for algebraic curves, and then motivate how the (classical) Riemann Roch
Theorem would be generalized to a result about sections on vector bundles

\section{Riemann Roch Problem}

The simplest case would be to deal with polynomial functions over $X = \C$ which are determined by a finite
set of roots (counting multiplicity) and a constant:
\[
  p(z) = c(z-a_1)\cdots (z-a_n)
\]
and similarly for rational functions.
% For future connection to sheaves let $\CO_X(X)$ represent all algebraic functions on $X$
% and $\CM_X(X)$ represent all rational functions on $X$; other common notation is $\C[X] = \CO_X(X)$ and $\C(X)
% = \CM_X(X)$ where $\C$ is often replaced with some other field $K$ or ring $R$.
If we ask for holomorphic
functions over $\C$, $(X,\CF) = (\C, \CH(\C))$, then the Weierstrass Factorization Theorem
(see~\cite[section~4.2]{NateComplexAnalysis}) tells us that $f \in \CH(\C)$ factors as an infinite polynomial
times a unit $e^{g(x}$ along with correction factors for the linear terms:
\[
  z^m e^{g(z)}\prod_k^\infty \left[ \left( 1- \frac{z}{a_k} \right)e^{p_k(z)} \right]
\]
where
\[
  p_k(z) =  \frac{z}{a_k} + \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{z}{a_k} \right)^2 + \cdots + \frac{1}{m_k} \left( \frac{z}{a_{m_k}} \right)^m
\]
is the correction factor and the $a_k$ are roots that form a non-accumulating sequence; in particular there are analytic concerns.
A meromorphic function $f$ on $\C$ can be determined by two entire function $f = g/h$ (again
see~\cite[section~4.2]{NateComplexAnalysis}); this is possible as for poles of $f$ we can define a function $h$ with the
appropriate roots and multiplicities. In other words, determining meromorphic functions becomes
a classification of non-accumulating sequences in $\C$. The situation requires even more analytic information in hyperbolic
space $D^2$ (think of the existence of $\sum_n z^{n!}$ and $1/(1-z)$). To understand the algebraic behaviour, limiting the scope to
\emph{compact spaces} such as Riemann surfaces or projective varieties gets much better as any holomorphic/meromorphic
functions must have finitely many roots/poles by the Identity Theorem (that if two functions agree on a set
of accumulation points, they are equal), and so we can focus our efforts within the algebraic setting. This
setting is plenty rich enough for study and often offers a good starting point to generalize to the
non-compact case\footnote{Though these cases are still very much being studied, see
\textcolor{red}{ref:HERE}}. In fact, by the efforts of Serre, Chow, and Grothendeick, the category of analytic
functions on (suitable) compact spaces is equivalent to the category of algebraic functions on these spaces
(\cite{hallGAGATheorems2022}, \cite{chowCompactComplexAnalytic1949}),
meaning compact spaces are ``intrinsically'' algebraic. We shall prove some of the pertinent parts of this
correspondence in~\ref{sec:gaga}.

Let us try finding holomorphic/meromorphic functions on $X = \CP^1$ and see if they are
determined by the points of $\CP^1$ just like in the case of $\C$. Immediately, $\CP^1$ has only constant holomorphic functions by an easy
generalization of Liouville or the maximum modulus principle on manifolds. By the Residue Theorem, the sum of
the number of roots and poles of a meromorphic function must be $0$.

Let us represent this formally. Let
\[
  D = \sum_i n_ip_i \qquad n_i\in \Z,\ p_i \in \CP^1
\]
be a finite formal sum; we may think of it as the free (abelian) group $F^{\Z}(\CP^1)$. Call $D$
a \emph{divisor}, and let $\Div(\CP^1)$
represent this group. A divisor can be thought of as an object that remembers which roots/poles with which
multiplicity we are interested in. Define:
\[
  \div(f) =  \sum_{p \in \CP^1} \nu_{p}(f)p
\]
where $\nu_{p}(f)$ is the order of the zero/pole, and is negative if it is a pole. This forms a subgroup of
$\Div(\CP^1)$; represent it as $\PDiv(\CP^1)$ (``P'' for principal), and elements of this subgroup are called
\emph{principal divisors}. This subgroup represents the divisors that are \emph{representable} as a
meromorphic function. Let us find which divisors can be represented. Let:
\[
  \deg D = \sum_i n_i
\]
Notice that $\deg(-)$ is certainly a group homomorphism. By our earlier remark:
\[
  \deg(\div(f)) = 0
\]
which is a restriction applied to any meromorphic function on $\CP^1$. It is easy to see that this restriction
would work on any compact Riemann surface (see~\cite[chapter~6]{NateComplexAnalysis}). If $\deg(D) = 0$, by
our earlier remark on the use of the Residue Theorem, we have that there exists an $f$ such that $\div(f) = D$,
showing this conditions characterizes meromorphic functions $f \in \CM(\CP^1)$ (but it shall not on other
Riemann surfaces, as demonstrated bellow).

Let us see to what extend the set of all divisors fail to represent a meromorphic function. Consider the
\emph{(Weil) divisor class group}:
\[
  \Cl(\CP^1) = \frac{\Div(\CP^1)}{\PDiv(\CP^1)}
\]
for any $[D] \in \Cl(\CP^1)$,  $\deg([D])$ is well-defined as $\deg(D) = \deg(D + f)$ for any $f \in
\PDiv(\CP^1)$. If $\deg(D) = \deg(D')$, then $\deg(D) - \deg(D) = \deg(D-D') = 0$, and hence $D-D'
= \div(f)$. Therefore, we have that:
\[
  \Cl(\CP^1) \cong \Z
\]
which can be thought of as measuring the obstruction in a meromorphic function being defined by its
points/roots.

Let us translate this intuition into sheaf cohomology, that is let us translate this into a  measures of
obstruction of local information having global information. The natural result we may want to think is that:
\[
  \Cl(\CP^1) \cong H^1(\CP^1, \CO_{\CP^1})
\]
However, $H^1(\CP^1, \CO_{\CP^1})$ is trivial. This because there is no obstruction to extending a function
\emph{additivity}, in particular if $\CP^1 = U_0 \cup U_1$ is the usual covering and if $f_{01} \in
\Gamma(U_0\cap U_1)$ then $f_{01} = g_0 - (-g_1)$ (decompose $f_{01}$ into a Laurent series, split the Laurent
series into the principal and non-principal part, change coordinates in the principal part so that it becomes
holomorphic and $U_1$). Instead, the obstruction is captured in the multiplicative structure\footnote{These
two are linked via a naturally exponential sequence $0 \to \Z \to \CO_X \to \CO_X^* \to 0$, the detail are
left for~\cite{NateAlgGeo}}:
\[
  \Cl(\CP^1) \cong H^1(\CP^1, \CO_{\CP^1}^*)
\]
The
higher order sheaf cohomology groups shall be trivial as the dimension of $\CP^1$ is $1$ (over $\C$), and
hence we'll have:
\begin{align*}
  H^0(\CP^1, \CO_{\CP^1}) &\cong \C\\
  H^1(\CP^1, \CO_{\CP^1}^*) &\cong \Cl(\CP^1)\\
  H^2(\CP^1, \CO_{\CP^1}) = H^2(\CP^1, \CO_{\CP^1}^*)&\cong \{0\}\\
  \vdots
\end{align*}
Doing the same when the result for $(X, \CF) = (\C, \CM_\C)$, it is not hard to see that:
\[
  \Cl(\C) = \frac{\Div(\C)}{\PDiv(\C)} \cong 0
\]
which shall give us:
\begin{align*}
  H^0(\C, \CO_{\C}) &\cong \C[x]\\
  H^1(\C, \CO_{\C}^*) &\cong \{0\}\\
  H^2(\C, \CO{(\C}) &\cong \{0\}\\
  \vdots
\end{align*}
as every divisor can be represented by a principal divisor. We can interpret this as there being no
impediments to using local information to construct meromorphic functions. Notice that the ring $\C[x]$ is
a UFD; this is the algebraic characterization of a trivial class group (details in~\cite{NateAlgGeo}), which
intuitively can be thought of as their being a ``unique'' way of constructing a function from local
information.

This answers the Riemann Roch problem for holomorphic/meromorphic functions for $\C$ and $\CP^1$. Let us look
at the next simplest nontrivial example. Let $X = \C/\Lambda$ where $\Lambda\sse \C$ is a lattice spanned by
the $\R$-linearly independent elements $(1,\tau)$ and let $\CF = \CM_X$ (all rational functions on $X$); $X$
is called an \emph{elliptic curve}, and so we shall label it as $E$. As $E$ is compact, if $f \in \CM_E$ then
$\div(f) = 0$. However, if $\deg(D) = 0$, this does not imply there exists an $f \in \CM_E$ such that $\div(f)
= D$. To see this, let's analyze $\CM_E$ a little more closely. As $\C/\Lambda$ is the quotient by $\Lambda$,
any meromorphic function must respect the $\Lambda$ structure:
\[
  f(z+n+m\tau) = f(z) \qquad \forall n+m\tau \in \Lambda
\]
such a function is called \emph{doubly periodic} or \emph{elliptic function} (as they are defined on elliptic
curves). Using this periodic condition, it is simple to see that
$\div(f) = 0$ by looking at:
\[
  \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D} \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}dz = 0
\]
where $D$ is the fundamental domain\footnote{If there are zero/poles on the path, we simply shift around
appropriately}, namely the opposing edges cancel out. We can modify this integral to find other properties,
for example:
\[
  \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D} z\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}dz = n + m\tau \in \Lambda
\]
which is nonzero as $z$ is not doubly periodic. Replacing $z$ by other algebraic functions all end up in
$\Lambda$, and so we see that if $D = \div(f)$:
\[
  \sum_i n_ip_i \equiv 0 \mod \Lambda
\]
where here $\sum_i n_ip_i\notin F^\Z(E)$ but $\sum_i n_ip_i \in E$, namely we are treating $p_i$ as a complex
number multiplied by $n_i$ and summed. Certainly there is no such restriction on
a general divisor $D \in \Div(E)$ of degree $0$. We thus have shown that there is a surjective map:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ellipticCrvIso}
  \Cl^0(E) = \frac{\Div^0(E)}{\PDiv(E)} \twoheadrightarrow \C/\Lambda
\end{equation}
where $\Div^0(E)$ are all divisor of degree $0$. It is in fact an isomorphism, which comes from constructing
an elliptic functions using the Weierstrass $\wp$-function, the details are
in~\cite[chapter~5]{NateComplexAnalysis}. Thus, we get the short exact sequence:
\[
  0 \to \C/\Lambda \to \Div(E) \xrightarrow{\deg} \Z \to 0
\]
often called the \emph{Jacobi-Abel} exact sequence as $\C/\Lambda$ is a Jacobian variety, and a short exact sequence:
\[
  0 \to \Cl^0(E) \to \Cl(E) \xrightarrow{\deg} \Z \to 0
\]
All together, we get a short exact sequence and a commutative diagram:
\[
  0 \to \PDiv(E) \to \Div^0(E) \to E \to 0
\]
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
0 \arrow[r] & \mathrm{Div}^0(E) \arrow[r, hook] \arrow[d, two heads, "\pi"] & \mathrm{Div}(E) \arrow[r, "\deg"] \arrow[d, two heads, "\pi"] & \mathbb{Z} \arrow[r] \arrow[d, equals] & 0 \\
0 \arrow[r] & \Cl^0(E) \arrow[r, hook] & \Cl(E) \arrow[r, "\deg"] & \mathbb{Z} \arrow[r] & 0
\end{tikzcd}
\]
Cohomology, as the surjective map in equation~(\ref{eq:ellipticCrvIso}) is an isomorphism:

\begin{align*}
  H^0(E, \CO_{E}) &\cong \C\\
  H^1(E, \CO_{E}^*) &\cong E\times \Z\\
  H^2(E, \CO_{E}) = H^2(E, \CO_{E}^*)&\cong \{0\}\\
  \vdots
\end{align*}

Continuing on to other Riemann surfaces, we shall loose the isomorphism given in
equation~(\ref{eq:ellipticCrvIso}), the obstruction to the map being an isomorphism is the beginning of the
study of Jacobian varieties (see~\cite[section~6.4]{NateComplexAnalysis}). The situation becomes even more
complicated going to higher dimensions. The above strategy using divisors to find cohomology information
becomes the beginning of the study of Weil and Cartier Divisors\footnote{and more generally chow rings
  (see~\cite{NateAlgGeo} and section~\ref{sec:GRR} on how this even characterizes rational cohomology under
suitable algebraic assumptions}; the study of Cartier/Weil divisors can be thought of as the study of the
obstruction of an object from being constructed from codimension $1$ objects, namely the study of $H^1(X,
\CO_X)$. This however does not help us in finding interesting holomorphic/meromorphic functions on $X$. Notice
further that this strategy only works on the simplest sheaves $\CO_X$, namely the structure sheaf and the
associated multiplicative sheaf $\CO_X^*$. Overall, we get some useful information about the obstruction to
finding global sections, but not necessarily concrete ways of finding $H^0(X, \CO_X)$.



To answer the Riemann Roch problem, a more granular approach to find which functions (of various kinds) exist
on a space $X$ must be taken. As divisors keep track of root/pole information, they are a great tool in
classifying functions that ``satisfy'' the properties of the divisor. To make this precise, define a patial ordering on
divisors $\div(X)$ where (for now) $X$ is either a Riemann surface or a smooth projective curve: let $D_2
\ge D_1$ if each coefficient of $D_2 - D_1$ is non-negative. Then we can consider:
\[
  \CO_X(D)(X) = \set{f \in \CM_X(X)^*}{\div(f) + D \ge 0}
\]
In other words, if $D =\sum_i n_ip_i$, then $\CO_X(D)(X)$ is all meromorphic functions $f$ where it has a pole
at $p_i$ of order at most $n_i$ if $n_i > 0$ and roots at $p_i$ of order at least $|n_j|$ if $n_j < 0$ (and
$\CO_X(D)$ is a [coherent] sheaf). From this, we can ask the Riemann Roch problem for $(X, \CO_X(D))$. This is
answered by the famous \emph{Riemann-Roch Theorem} where Riemann proved the Riemann-inequality, and Roch
provided the necessary work to make it an equality, see~\cite{a.griffithsIntroductionAlgebraicCurves}. Due to
its generalizations, it is now known as \emph{Riemann-Roch for curves} and is the equality:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:riemannRoch1dIntro}
  \dim H^0(X, \CO_X(D)) - \dim H^1(X, \CO_X(D)) = \deg(D)+1-g
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the genus of the Riemann surface or smooth projective curve
(see~\cite{hartshorneAlgebraicGeometry2013} or~\cite{NateAlgGeo} for defining the genus of a curve via scheme
theory, or for a
direct computational proof using normalization see~\cite{NateComplexAnalysis}). In the original phrasing of the Riemann Roch
theorem, the group $H^1(X, \CO_X(D))$ is replaced with  $H^0(X, \CO(K_X-D))^\vee$ where $K_X$ is the canonical
divisor of $X$; these groups are isomorphic by Serre Duality (see~\cite[section~6.3]{NateAlgGeo}). Then this
space is identified with the space of meromorphic $1$-forms (either through some simple techniques from
complex analysis, see Brill-Noether Reciprocity in either~\cite{a.griffithsIntroductionAlgebraicCurves}
or~\cite{NateComplexAnalysis}, or by combining of Poincar\'e duality with DeRham's Theorem, see
either~\cite{leeIntroductionRiemannianManifolds2018} or~\cite{NateDiffGeo}). Switching to the traditional
notation $l(D) = \dim H^0(X, \CO_X(D))$ and $i(D) = \dim H^0(X, \CO_X(K_X-D))^*$ we get:
\[
  l(D) - l(K_X-D) = \deg(D) + 1-g \qquad \text{(equiv.)} \qquad l(D) - i(D) = \deg(D) + 1-g
\]
This result will be proven in~\cref{th:rrForRiemannSurfacewithLineBundles}. As we phrased the Riemann-Roch
problem in the beginning, this formula would then be asking for the value of $l(D)$. There are cases where
$l(K_X-D)$ disappears (for example if $D = 0$ or $D = K_X$), and there are many cases we can compute it easily
enough. It is simple to compute the right hand side but in general it is harder to computer the left hand
side. Thus, this formula becomes a new constriction that helps us find $l(D)$. There are a set of theorems
known as \emph{vanishing theorems} that specify under which conditions $l(K_X-D)$ (or more generally, higher
cohomology groups) vanish (covered in section~\ref{sec:Kodaira}, but for all the details
see~\cite{deligneRelevementsModulop2etDecomposition1987}); under these conditions we recover $l(D)$ without
a ``fudge factor''\footnote{The term ``fudge factor'' was common vocabulary in the Italian school of the 19th century in
the context of intersection theory, and due to the work of Fulton (\cite{fultonIntersectionTheory1984}) the
term is applicable to the cohomological context via the cycle map}.


This formula works for Riemann surface/projective curves. Let us work towards the intuition behind the
generalization. The notation on left hand side in equation~(\ref{eq:riemannRoch1dIntro}) can be compactified
by using the Euler-Poincar\'e characteristic (or simply the Euler characteristic): $\chi(X, \CO_X(D)) =
\sum_{i=0}^\infty (-1)^i \dim H^i(X, \CO_X(D))$ so that:
\[
  \chi(X, \CO_X(D)) = \deg(D) + 1-g
\]
As $X$ is a curve $H^i(X, \CO_X(D)) = 0$ for $i > 1$\footnote{This is a very common fact and can be proven  in
  multiple different ways. Algebraic proof: \cite[section~28.3]{NateAlgGeo}, topological
proof:\cite[chapter~3]{NateAlgTop}, geometric proof:\cite[chapter~3]{NateKTheory}}   giving the equality. The
right hand side shall be drastically different, split into special invariants of vector bundles known as the
\emph{Chern character} and \emph{Todd class} where the Chern character can be thought of as capturing some ``algebraic'' or
``local'' information given by $D$ (this will generalize the $\deg(D)$) while the Todd class shall capture the
``geometric'' or ``global'' information (this will generalize the $1+g$). In fact, the Euler Characteristic
can easily be generalized to be an invariant on vector bundles by using sheaf cohomology instead of singular
cohomology, and hence the Riemann Roch theorem generalizes to a question about the existence of sections on
vector bundles over a compact manifolds, namely if $(X,\CF)$ is a compact manifold along with a vector bundle
$\CF$, then we want to compute:
\[
  H^0(X, \CF)
\]
Note that there is a bijective correspondence between vector bundle and locally free sheaves
(see~\cite[chapter~4]{NateAlgGeo} for the details), and hence we may treat $\CF$ as either a vector bundle or
a sheaf; the details for line bundles is given in section~\ref{sec:whyVecBundles}. The result of this will be:
\[
  \chi(X, \CF) = \int_X \ch(X)\td(X)
\]
This equation is precisely the result of the \emph{Hirzebruch-Riemann Roch Theorem}. We shall mention the
generalization to smooth quasi-projective schemes over a field near the end, and also talk a bit about a
recent paper (\cite{navarroRiemannRochFormulaProjective2017}) generalizing to finite dimensional Noetherian
schemes.

\subsection{How do Vector Bundles enter the Picture}\label{sec:whyVecBundles}

To motivate how vector bundles will enter the picture, it is worth-while seeing how $\CO_X(D)$ can be
interpreted as a line bundle. To that end, recall that the projective curve $X = \P^1$ as defined in classical
algebraic geometry only has constant global sections. Namely, if $U_0, U_1\cong \A^1$ is the usual cover with
global sections (i.e. functions) $\CO_X(U_0) \cong \CO_X(U_1) = K[x]$ and the compatibility condition on
$U_0\cap U_1$ is $f(z) = g(1/z)$, namely:
\begin{center}
  \[
\begin{tikzcd}
U_0                                               &               U_1 \\
     U_0\cap U_1 \arrow[r, "\cong", no head] \arrow[u, hook] & U_0\cap U_1 \arrow[u, hook]
\end{tikzcd}
\qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad
\begin{tikzcd}
\A^1                                       &              \A^1 \\
     \A^1\setminus\{0\} \arrow[u, hook] \arrow[r, "\cong", no head] & \A^1\setminus\{0\} \arrow[u, hook]
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{center}
gives:
\begin{center}
  \[
\begin{tikzcd}
\CO_X(U_0) \arrow[d]                                &              \CO_X(U_1) \arrow[d] \\
                    \CO_X(U_0\cap U_1) \arrow[r, "\cong", no head] & \CO_X(U_0\cap U_1)
\end{tikzcd}
\qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad
\begin{tikzcd}
{K[x]} \arrow[d]                        & {K[y]} \arrow[d] \\
{K[x, 1/x]} \arrow[r, "\cong", no head] & {K[y, 1/y]}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{center}
where $x \mapsto 1/y$ and $1/x \mapsto y$, Then if $F([x':y']) \in
\CO_{\P^1}(\P^1)$ is a global function where $x = x'/y'$ and $y  =y'/x'$ (when $y',x'\ne 0$ respectively), then $F([x:1]) \to f(x) \in \CO_X(U_0)$ and $F([1:y]) \to g(y) \in \CO_X(U_1)$
are the restrictions of the function to $U_0$ and $U_1$ respectively, and by the above diagram it must be that
these functions map to the same element in the restriction, namely:
\[
  f(x) \to f(x) = g(1/x) = g(y) \leftarrow g(y)
\]
but it is impossible that $f(x) = g(1/x)$ in $k[x]$ unless $f,g$ are the same constant. Thus, it must be that the only
global functions on the projective line are constants:
\[
  \CO_{\P^1}(\P^1) = K
\]
The fundamental limitation here is that the restriction $f(x) = g(1/x)$ is very strong. What if we can loosen
this restriction? For example what if
\[
  f(x) = xg\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)
\]
Then we can in fact form linear homogeneous functions! For example consider
\[
  F([x':y']) = ax'+by'
\]
Then $F$ maps to $f(x) = ax+b$ and $g(y) = a + by$. Then:
\[
  f(x)= ax+b = x\left(a + b \frac{1}{x}\right)  = xg\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)
\]
which works! More generally, if the condition $f(x) = x^ng(1/x)$ allows for degree $n$ homogeneous
polynomials, and the homogeneity can happen for any number of variables. Denote the degree $n$-homogeneous
polynomials for $\P^k$ by $\CO_{\P^k}(n)(\P^k)$.

Now, recall that a $n$-dimensional vector bundle $\pi: E \to X$ over a field $K$ can be defined by taking open
sets $U_i \sse X$ such that $\pi^{-1}(U_i) \to U_i\times F$ and insuring that for any intersection $U_i\cap
U_j$ we have a gluing map:
\[
  g_{ij}: \pi^{-1}(U_i\cap U_j) \to \GL_n(K)
\]
such that $g_{jk}g_{ij} = g_{ik}$ (the cocycle condition). When limiting
to line bundles, the gluing map becomes:
\[
  g_{ij}: \pi^{-1}(U_i\cap U_j) \to K^*
\]
Intuitively, this give a point-wise gluing conditions on the bundle: if $v \in U_i$ and $w \in U_j$ that are
the same point in $U_i\cap U_j$, then:
\[
  v = A_{ij}w
\]
Now, let us correspond $\CO_{\P^1}(n)$ to a line bundle $\CL(n)$. Let $\CL(n)$ be
locally trivial on $U_1, U_2$: $\Gamma(U_i, \CL(n)) = \CO_{\CP^1}(n)(U_i)$. On their intersection define the gluing
map: \[ g_{ij}: \CO_{\P^1}(U_i\cap U_j) \to \CO_{\P^1}^*(U_i\cap U_j) \cong k\left[x,
\frac{1}{x}\right]\setminus\{0\} \] where we take $\CO_{\P^1}^*(U_i\cap U_j)$ in place of $K^*$ as these are
the ``units'' (i.e. isomorphisms) on this intersection. For a given $f,g$ to restrict to the same functions on
the intersection, we now ``twist'' $g$ to be:

\[
  f(x) = x^ng(1/x) \qquad x^n \in \CO_{\P^1}^*(U_i\cap U_j)
\]
The word ``twist'' comes from both complex analysis where $z^n$ twists $\C$ onto itself around the branching
point, and from algebraic geometry where the non-triviality of bundles is partly captured by characteristic
classes that represent the obstruction to some non-trivial global trivialization via twisting (we shall cover
this intuition in chapter~\ref{cha:characteristicClass}).

This gives how we can construct a line bundle $\CL(n)$ for sheaves $\CO_{\CP^1}(n)$. The converse works as
well: the details of the correspondence is given in~\cite[section~4.1]{NateAlgGeo} (especially how to
transition from points of a bundle to section of a sheaf). Overall, we see that $\CO_{\P^1}(n)(\P^1)$ is the
global sections of a line bundle; more generally $\CO_{\P^1}(n)$ remember all the sections over each open set
$U$, as $\CO_{\P^1}(n)$ is a sheaf.

With this intuition, we can see how to interpret $\CO_X(D)$ as a line bundle. Choosing an affine cover $U_i$
for $X$, we can define:
\[
  D|_{U_i} = \sum_{p \in U_i} n_pp
\]
which is the representation of the divisor $D$ in $U_i$. Then for each affine $U_i$, choose $s_i \in \CM_X(U_i)$
so that $\langle s_i \rangle_K = \CO_X(D)(U_i)$ is a generator as a $K$-algebra of the local sections and:
\[
  \div(s_i) = -D|_{U_i}
\]
So that $s_i$ has roots/poles of the appropriates order. Such a section exists, for $U_i \cong \A^1$ (as $X$
is a curve) so $\CO_X(U_i) \cong k[x]$ and so by our earlier discussion we can certainly find an $s_i$ with
exactly zeros and poles at $-D|_{U_i}$ and it is an exercise in ring-theory to show this is a generator (think
of choosing a uniformization parameter for a local ring).

Let us label the collection of these meromorphic functions $\CO_X(D)(U_i)$. To find an $f \in \CO_X(D)(X)$ (i.e.
a global section), we need to insure we can glue together nicely on intersection. Consider (nonempty)
$U_i\cap U_j$ and take the generators $s_i \in \CO_X(D)(U_i)$ and $s_j \in \CO_X(D)(U_j)$. It is not hard to
see that $s_i|_{U_i\cap U_j} =
s_j|_{U_i\cap U_j}$. Then certainly on $U_i\cap U_j$:
\[
  \div(s_i) = \div(s_j) = -D|_{U_i\cap U_j}
\]
and so, as we are considering meromorphic functions, we can consider:
\[
  \div \left(\frac{s_j}{s_i}\right) = \div(s_j) - \div(s_i) = 0
\]
As $s_j/s_i$ has no zero's/pole on $U_i\cap U_j$, this means that it is holomorphic and nowhere vanishing on
$U_i\cap U_j$. But then:
\[
  g_{ij} = \frac{s_j}{s_i} \in \CO_X^*(U_i\cap U_j)
\]
Showing that we have the desired transition functions to define a line bundle! Thus, we may glue them to form
all global sections $\CO_{X}(D)(X)$ for every $D$, and then $\CO_X(D)$ is a line bundle. In most cases (and in
most spaces that will be considered in this paper), every line bundle $\CL$ is induced by a divisor $D$;
\cite[section~4.3]{NateAlgGeo} shows how there is a bijective correspondence between line bundles and
(Weil/Cartier) divisors on the appropriate type of scheme.



\subsection{Motivating Euler Characteristic}

The origins of the Euler characteristic stem from an observation by Leonhard Euler about the relation between
the vertices, edges, and faces of a convex Polyhedron. If $V,E,F$ represents the respective quantities, then:
\[
  V-E+F = 2
\]
For example, a cube has 8 vertices, 12 edges, and 6 faces, so $8-12+6 = 2$. Similarly, a closed connected
planar graph holds the same property (counting the outside of the planar graph as a face). This sum is called
the \emph{Euler Characteristic} of the polyhedron. If the polyhedron was not convex, this relation need not
hold, however it is possible to have a non-convex polyhedron whose Euler characteristic is $2$. This shows
that the Euler characteristic capture some rough information about the geometry of the shape.


Generalizing this, we can form CW-complex $C$ on a topological space $X$ and define the Euler characteristic
as:
\[
  \chi(X, C) = \sum_{i=0}^\infty (-1)^ik_i
\]
where $k_i$ is the number of CW-complexes per dimension. One of the crowning results of algebraic topology in
the 21st century is the invariance of this number of choice of CW complex. This lead to the development of
\emph{singular cohomology} with the homology group $H_i(X, \Z)$ and the \emph{Betti numbers} $b_i = \rk(H_i(X,
\Z))$ and:
\[
  \chi(X) = \sum_{i=0}^\infty (-1)^ib_i = \sum_{i=0}^\infty (-1)^i\rk(H_i(X, \Z))
\]
As this is a homological constant, it is often called the \emph{Euler-Poincar\'e} characteristic to
distinguish it from the Euler characteristic for polyhedron, but we shall rarely make this distinction. As
cohomology groups are invariant up to homotopy on $X$, the Euler characteristic is invariant to homotopy. It
is often computationally easier to work with the cohomology group, and by the Poincar\'e duality
\cite[section~4.3]{NateAlgTop} (or its generalization, Serre duality~\cite[section~5.3]{NateAlgGeo}), we have
the equivalent sum:
\[
  \chi(X) =  \sum_{i=0}^\infty (-1)^i\rk(H^i(X, \Z))
\]
How should we interpret the alternating nature of the sum when summing over homology/cohomology? The key idea
is to notice that the alternating sum mimics the pattern when invoking the inclusion-exclusion principle.
Looking at the Euler characteristic for polyhedron, we can take for example a cube, and then we can think of
each face as including the vertices and edges, and the edges as including the vertices. By counting all the
faces, we have in fact double counted the edges. We thus subtract the sum of the edges. But this shall at the
same time remove all the vertices from the sum, and hence we add them back in. This is exactly the
inclusion-exclusion principle.

When converting to a cohomological perspective, we recover the inclusion-exclusion principle when taking
\emph{\v{C}ech cohomology}. In particular, the intuition is to cover your space $X$ in open sets, define the
desired type of functions on each open set, and measure the obstruction to gluing these functions on
intersections, intersection of intersections, and so on and so forth:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large]
\check{C}^0(\mathcal{U}, \CF) \arrow[r, "d^0"] & \check{C}^1(\mathcal{U}, \CF) \arrow[r, "d^1"] & \check{C}^2(\mathcal{U}, \CF) \arrow[r, "d^2"] & \cdots \\
\prod_{i} \CF(U_i) \arrow[u, equal] \arrow[r, "\delta^0"] & \prod_{i<j} \CF(U_i \cap U_j) \arrow[u, equal] \arrow[r, "\delta^1"] & \prod_{i<j<k} \CF(U_i \cap U_j \cap U_k) \arrow[u, equal] \arrow[r, "\delta^2"] & \cdots
\end{tikzcd}
\]

How should we interpret what invariance is captured by the Euler characteristic? The case of the convex and
non-convex polyhedron hints at a notion of curvature, and indeed by the Gauss-Bonnet theorem a Riemann surface
$X$ (without boundary) with Riemann metric $\omega$ which induces a Gaussian curvature $K$ can be computer by
taking:
\[
  \chi(M) = \frac{1}{2\pi }\int_M K dA
\]
and generalizes to higher-dimensional surfaces. Another interpretation from the theory of vector fields is
that that number of zeros/poles as well as point with non-trivial curve are given by the topology of the
surface, and hence captured by $\chi$ (see~\cite{NateAlgTop}).

These are very useful invariants, especially in $2$ dimension where the classification of Riemann surfaces up
to homotopy if first broken down into the case where the Euler characteristic is positive, zero, or negative
(representing spherical, flat, and hyperbolic curvature respectively), and these values are strongly tied to
the properties of vector fields on the surface. However, we see that the Euler characteristic can be defined
on any space admitting a cohomology theory, suggesting the vector-field interpretation may be more suitable. The
following section covers these ideas.

\subsection{Motivating Characteristic Theory}\label{sec:motivatingCharTheory}

By the discussion in section~\ref{sec:whyVecBundles}, $\deg(D)$ and $1-g$ should be interpreted as a property
of a bundle. Let us return to the Gauss-Bonnet Theorem to find the motivation of this generalization. Recall
that if $M$ is a compact two-dimensional Riemannian manifold with empty and $K$ is be the Gaussian curvature
of $M$, then:
\[
  \chi(M) = \frac{1}{2\pi }\int_M K dA
\]
It was proven by Chern (the \emph{Chern Theorem} or \emph{Chern-Gauss-Bonnet Theorem}) that this generalizes
to an even dimensional closed Riemann manifold to the curvature class of the Euler form:
\[
  \chi(M) = \int_M e(\Omega)
\]
where $e$ is the \emph{Euler class} (which is characteristic class), and $\Omega$ is the 2-form representing
curvature which can be embedded in: \[
  \bigwedge^{2n} T^*M
\]
More generally, if $M$ is any smooth orientable $n$-dimensional manifold then:
\[
  \chi(M) = (e(TM), [M])
\]
where $[M]$ is the fundamental class and $(-,-)$ is the cap product. Thus, the Euler characteristic $\chi$ is
already strongly linked to characteristic theory as it can be seen as the perfect pairing between the Euler
class and the fundamental class. The details of the definition of the Euler class can be seen
in~\cite{NateKTheory}, the important point to remember is that it is the given by taking the \emph{top Chern
class} of a manifold $M$ and a modified tangent bundle $E_{TM}$,

This definition does not consider non-trivial vector bundles, in particular when $M$ is closed then $\chi(M)$
can be considered as the Euler characteristic over the trivial bundle $\mathbbm{1}$ as:
\[
  H^i(M, \mathbbm{1}) = H^i(M, \Z)
\]
By considering different bundles $E$, using sheaf cohomology we can define:
\[
  \chi(M, E) = \sum_i^\infty (-1)^i \dim H^i(M, \CO_E)
\]
The extra non-trivial information within the cohomology groups introduce by $E$ motivates the focus on Chern
classes for the generalization.


Thus, let us motivate what the Chern classes represent. First off, the natural transformation $K_k(-) \Rw H^*$
shows that the Chern class $c$ can is a sum of $i$th Chern classes, one for each $H^i$. Next, as $H^i$
vanishes when $i > \dim(M)$, there can be at most finitely many $i$th Chern classes. As we are mapping into
the cohomology ring, the Chern class will represent some obstruction information. Given a (complex) vector
bundle $E$ of dimension, it is natural to ask whether it possible to define a $k$ frames $1\le k \le n$ on $E$
that are linearly independent. The existence of an nontrivial $i$th Chern class shall represent the there is
an obstruction to having a $n-i$ globally linearly independent frames, in particular it will not be possible
to extend $n-i$ linearly independent frames to a $n-i+1$ linearly independent frame. As this is a
vector-bundle dependent invariant, the Chern classes represent some algebraic complexity of $E$ in terms of
the cohomology of $M$.

Given a Chern class $c_i \in H^{2i}(M, \Z)$ (the $2i$ shall be explained in
chapter~\ref{cha:characteristicClass}), what would evaluating $c_i$ represent? Given a representative $T$ of
a homology class $H_{2i}(M, \Z)$, we can define the \emph{Chern number} $c_i(E)$ at $T$ to be:
\[
  \langle c_i(E), [T] \rangle = \int_T c_i(E)
\]
The Chern number counts the number of zeros/poles of a generic section of the vector bundle $E$, restricted to the
submanifold $T$ (see~\cite{NateKTheory}). In terms of the earlier curvature interpretation, it is around
zero or poles (where poles can be considered as zero's in the reverse orientation) that we accumulate non-trivial
curling/divergence. Let us apply this intuition where $M$ is a Riemann surface and $E$ a line bundle on $M$.
Then $i = 1$ so that $c_1(\CL(D)) \in H^2(M, \Z)$, for a line bundle $\CL(D)$ associated to $D$. Then by the
above intuition, we see that:
\[
  \int_D c_1(\CL(D)) = \deg(D)
\]
In a sense, this is the ``algebraic'' part of the Riemann Roch theorem. If we let $D = 0$, then:
\[
  \chi(M) = 1-g
\]
To get this information from Chern classes, notice that as we can always define the tangent bundle $TM$ of
$M$, we can compute $c_1(TM)$. Then by the \emph{Poincar\'e-Hopf Theorem} (\cite{NateAlgTop}
or~\cite{NateDiffGeo}) we get that $c_1(TM) = 2-2g$. Thus we get:
\[
  \chi(M) = \frac{1}{2}c_1(TM)
\]
It should be noted that in this case, the Euler class is equal to the top Chern class, $c_1(TM) = e(TM)$, and
so in this low-dimension case we could have just applied the Gauss-Bonnet Theorem.

When moving up to higher dimensions, it is important to limit to smooth (complete) varieties, namely because the higher
cohomological information on closed $n$-manifolds where $n\ge 2$ holds ``more'' information than there are
geometric sub-manifolds; limiting to smooth varieties (and later proven by Grothendeick to proper
schemes) shall restrict the cohomology groups enough to continue this pattern. To tie the geometric properties
of manifolds to smooth varieties, we shall show in section~\ref{sec:kahlerMan} what conditions must be added
on a manifold $M$ so that it has to be a smooth variety. For now, we shall simply assume $M$ is a smooth
variety.

For those who know the Riemann-Roch theorem for surfaces (ex. see~\cite[chapter~5.1,
theorem~1.6]{hartshorneAlgebraicGeometry2013})
\[
\chi(D) = \chi(0) + \frac{1}{2}D.(D-K)
\]
they can verify that:
\[
  \chi(M, \CL(D)) =\frac{1}{2}c_1(\CL(D))^2 + \frac{1}{2}c_1(\CL(D))c_1(TM) + \frac{1}{12}(c_1(TM)^2 + c_2(TM))
\]
showing that the ability to write $\chi$ in terms of $TM$ and $\CL(D)$ does extend non-trivially to higher
dimensions.


So the goal now is to find a connection between the Euler-characteristic and some equation of Chern classes.
Two important decomposition of the Euler characteristic is:
\[
  \chi(M\oplus M', E)) = \chi(M, E)\cdot \chi(M', E)
\]
which is a standard cohomology proof when $E = \mathbbm{1}$, and in~\cref{th:generalizedEulerCharAdditive} we shall show that:
\[
  \chi(M, E\oplus E') = \chi(M, E) + \chi(M, E')
\]
Thus, we shall need two invariance to take into account the different algebraic behaviour of the manifold and

algebraic information for any manifold $M$ and vector bundle $E$: we shall show that:
\[
  \ch(E\oplus E') = \ch(E) + \ch(E')
\]
for the geometric counter-part, it was \emph{John Arthur Todd} who found

If we were working with trivial bundles, then we can simply write:

\chapter{Necessary Vector Bundle Theory}\label{cha:characteristicClass}

This chapter will cover the necessary vector/fiber bundle theory that is used in Hirzebruch Riemann Roch. For
an in-depth reading, there are my notes, \cite{NateKTheory} that also include the algebraic $K$-theory
background necessary to generalize the results of this chapter to Fulton's Intersection Theory. For
a different reference, I recommend Hatcher's textbook on vector bundles \cite{hatcherVectorBundlesKTheory2017}
where he also covers geometric $K$-theory. Furthermore, this chapter shall freely use the bijective
correspondence between locally free sheaves and vector bundles, and may convert between the language of sheaf
theory and bundle theory without much warning.

Let $E \to X$ be vector bundle, where $X$ is paracompact and finite dimensional (either as a manifold or
topologically)\footnote{these conditions insure we get bijective maps between the cohomological spaces of
sheaves and the underlying space}. A natural set of questions to ask is what are the possible (global)
sections on $E$ with respect to $X$. A classical example of this question is to ask if there are any
non-vanishing global sections on $TS^2$ with respect to $S^2$; the \emph{Hedgehog Theorem} (or infamously, the
\emph{Hairy Ball Theorem}) asserts that
no such global section can exist~\cite{NateAlgTop}. As local sections are easy to produce by taking advantage
of local trivialization, the question is what type of \emph{obstructions} are there in producing a global
sections. This is thus a cohomological problem.
% and characteristic theory shows that there is a natural
% functor from vector bundles to the cohomology ring of the base space that quantifies this information.

By the work of mathematicians such as Chern, Thom, Whitney, and many others, there are natural transformations
from the Vector-bundle functor to the cohomology functor of the underlying space:
\[
  c: K_k(-) \Rw H^*
\]
where $K_k(X)$ is the isomorphism classes of $k$-vector bundles over $X$, and $k$ is a field (usually $\R$
or $\C$). Without much trouble, this can be generalized to principal $G$-bundles; this shall be done for the
important case of Flag bundles and Grassmannians that will allow for both classification results and
computational results. In this case, a \emph{characteristic class} would be a natural transformation:
\[
  c: b_G(-) \Rw H^*
\]
where $b_G(-)$ is the functor taking $X$ to the isomorphism of principal $G$-bundles over $X$. Note that there
can be more than one characteristic class. Uncurling the categorical language, these natural
transformations associate (up to isomorphism\footnote{even up to homotopy, see~\cite{NateKTheory}}) to each principal $G$ bundle $P \to X\in b_G(X)$ an element $c(P) \in H^*$ such
that
\[
  c(f^*(P)) = f^*(c(P))
\]
that is, the pullback of the vector bundle commutes with the pullback of
cohomology classes. The characteristic classes will be the ``right'' notion to capture the cohomological
information, in particular when working over real and complex vector bundles, the cohomology
of the classifying space ($BO$ for real, $BU$ for complex) is given by the \emph{Stiefel-Whitney} and \emph{Chern} classes
(see~\cite{NateKTheory}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:naturalCharClas}
  H^*(BO; \Z/2\Z) \cong \Z_2[w_1, ..., w_n] \qquad \text{and} \qquad  H^*(BU; \Z)
  \cong \Z[c_1, ..., c_n]
\end{equation}
In this paper, our base spaces are (complete) varieties\footnote{in section~\ref{sec:GRR} we shall extend to
proper schemes}, so we shall mainly focus on complex vector bundles and principal
$\GL_n(\C)$-bundles. The complex case works with \emph{Chern class} and the \emph{Pontryagin class}: the Chern class
is the natural characteristic class of complex vector bundles (as seen in
equation~(\ref{eq:naturalCharClas})), while the Pontryagin class can be thought of as the characteristic
classes of the complexification of a real vector bundle.

The Chern class is central to the Hirzebruch Riemann Roch as they are directly used in defining the Chern
character and the Todd class. On the other hand, the Pontryagin classes shall be useful in that they will
allow us to fully classify all cobordant oriented manifolds, a result that will be necessary for only requiring checking
$\chi$ agrees with $\int_X \ch(E)\td(T_X)$ on complex projective space.

Another characteristic class that shall not be covered but is important to know as it is one of the
generalizations of the Euler characteristic is the \emph{Euler class}. As
the name suggests, the Euler class is linked to the Euler characteristic, in particular if $M$ is an oriented
closed $n$-manifold, $TM$ the tangent bundle, $e(TM) \in H^n(M; \Z)$, and $[M] \in H_n(M; \Z)$ the fundamental class of $M$, then:
\[
  e(TM) \frown [M] = \chi(M)
\]
or, given the DeRham isomorphism (see~\cite{NateDiffGeo}), re-labeling the notation $e(TM)$ to the corresponding \emph{Euler form}:
\[
  \int_M e(TM) = \chi(M)
\]
If $M$ where given a Riemann metric, then the classical generalization of the Gauss-Bonnet theorem for
manifolds without boundary (the \emph{Chern Theorem}) let's us replace $TM$ with $\Omega$, the curvature form,
giving:
\[
  \int_M e(\Omega) = \chi(M)
\]
In other words, the Chern Theorem finds an alternative way of evaluating the Euler class at the fundamental
class that relies on the curvature form $\Omega$. The Hirzebruch Riemann Roch can be seen as
finding another alternative evaluation at the Euler class when the underlying space $M$ has enough structure for
its characterization to be done using the Chern classes.

Finally, the definition of Chern class and Pontryagin class taken here can be thought of as the ``classical''
definition. In algebraic geometry, there is natural ``intersection theory'' perspective of of the Chern class
that maps the chow group $A^n(-)$ to $A^{n-k}(-)$. There is a natural way in which this definition is linked
to the classical definition of Chern class via the Chern character which shall be covered in
\textcolor{red}{ref:HERE}, and the we shall briefly bring it up in the context of the generalization of the
Hirzebruch Riemann Roch to the \emph{Grothendeick Riemann Roch theorem} (see \textcolor{red}{ref:HERE}) to
show an important representation of the $K_0(X)$ group using chow groups.


\section{Definitions and key Results}

For reference, here is the definition of a vector bundle and Principal bundle we shall use (this differs from
the definition Hirzebruch gives via gluing of a vector bundle with structure groups, but matches contemporary
textbooks such as those by Hatcher and John/Jeffrey M. Lee):

\index{Fiber Bundle}\index{Bundle!Fiber}
\begin{defn}{Fiber Bundle}{fibBundle}
	Let $B$ be a paracompact topological space. Then a quadruple $(B, E, \pi, F)$ where $E$ is
	a topological space called the \emph{total space}, $\pi: E \to B$ is a continuous surjection called the
	\emph{bundle projection}, and $B$ is called the \emph{base space}, and $F$ is the fiber is called
	\emph{fiber bundle} if:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item (regular fibers) each $\pi^{-1}(b)$ for $b \in B$ is isomorphic to $F$, or in particular
			\[
				\pi^{-1}(b) \cong b\times F
			\]
		\item (local trivialization)For each $ b\in B$, there exists an open set $U \sse B$ containing $b$ such that the following
			diagram commutes
			\[
				\begin{tikzcd}
				\pi^{-1}(U) \arrow[r, "\sim"] \arrow[d, "\pi"'] & U\times F \arrow[ld, "\pi_1"] \\
				U                                               &
				\end{tikzcd}
			\]
	\end{enumerate}
	When clear from context, the fiber bundle is usually denoted as $E$.
\end{defn}

\index{Structure Group}\index{G-bundle}
\begin{defn}{G-bundle}{GBundle}
  Let $E \to X$ be a fiber bundle and $G$ a topological that acts faithfully on the fibers. Then
  a \emph{$G$-atlas} is a collection of local trivializations $\{U_i, \phi_i\}_i$ such that for any index
  $i,j$ on the overlap $U_i\cap U_j$ (if it is non-empty):
  \begin{gather*}
    \phi_j^{-1}\circ \phi_i: (U_i\cap U_j)\times F \to (U_i\cap U_j) \times F \\
    \phi_j^{-1}\circ\phi_i(p, f) = (p, g_{ij}(p)f)
  \end{gather*}
  with the added conditions that:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $g_{11} = \id$
    \item $g_{ij} = g_{ji}^{-1}$
    \item $g_{ik} = g_{jk}g_{ij}$
  \end{enumerate}
  Two $G$-atlases are equivalent if their union is a $G$-atlas. A \emph{G-bundle} is a fiber bundle with an
  equivalence relation of $G$-atlases.
\end{defn}


\index{Principal Bundle}\index{Bundle!Principal}
\begin{defn}{Principal $G$-Bundle}{principalBundle}
	If $F = G$ is a topological and $P \to X$ is a fiber bundle over $X$ group where there is a free and
	transitive continuous
	right group action $P\times G \to P$ that preserves the fibers of $P$, then it is called a \emph{principal
	G-bundle} (or \emph{principal bundle} if $G$ is clear from context. In other words, a principal $G$-bundle
  is a $G$-bundle where the fibers are $G$.

	\qn
	Each fiber is called a \emph{G-torsor} (which is a space that where the action $G\actson F_x$ has trivial
	stabilizer, hence ``is'' the group $G$ but does not intrinsically have a notion of identity element)
\end{defn}

	fiber-morphism in the local trivialization is a linear isomorphism, then is called a \emph{vector bundle}

\begin{thm}{Homotopy Invariance Vector Bundles}{homotopyInvarianceVecBundles}

	\[
		f_0^*(E) \cong f_1^*(E)
	\]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  \cite[section~1.2]{NateKTheory}
\end{Proof}

\index{Classifying Vector Bundles}
\begin{thm}{Classifying Vector Bundles}{classifyingVecBundles}
	Let $X$ be a paracompact space. Then $K^n(X)$ is in correspondence with the set of homotopy
	classes of maps $X \to \Grass_n$, denoted $[X, \Grass_n]$. In other words:
	\[
		K^n(X) \cong [X,\Grass_n]
	\]
	In particular, if $E \to X$ is an $n$-dimensional vector bundle, it is isomorphic to $f^*(E_n)$ for some
	$f: X \to \Grass_n$ where $E$ determines $f$ up to homotopy.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  \cite[section~1.2]{NateKTheory}
\end{Proof}

\section{The splitting Principle}

One the most important results about vector bundles is that there is a pullback map which allows them to split
into a direct sum of line bundles. As a consequence, the total Chern class (which is representable as
a polynomial) can be split into product of Chern classes of line bundles (which can be represented by linear
terms). The key cohomological result is the following ``fiber-local to global'' theorem proved by Leray and
Hirsch:

\begin{thm}{Leray-Hirsch Theorem}{leray-HirschThm}
	Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $p: E \to X$ be a fiber bundle. In~\cite{NateAlgTop} it was shown $H^*(E;R)$
	is a $H^*(X;R)$-module where
	\[
		\alpha\cdot \beta := p^*(\alpha)\smile \beta
	\]
	with $\alpha \in H^*(X)$ and $\beta \in H^*(E)$. If
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item for each fiber $F$ the inclusion $F \hookrightarrow E$ induces a surjection on $H^*(-;R)$
		\item $H^n(F;R)$ is a free $R$-module of finite rank for each $n$.
	\end{enumerate}
	Then $H^*(E;R)$ is a free $H^*(X;R)$-module. A basis for the $H^*(X;R)$-module $H^*(E;R)$ can be obtained
	by choosing any set of elements in $H^*(E;R)$ that map to a basis for $H^*(F;R)$ under the map induced by
	the inclusion.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
	see~\cite{NateAlgTop}.
\end{Proof}


\begin{thm}{Splitting Principle}{splittingPrinciple}
	Let $\pi: E \to B$ be a complex vector bundle. Then there exists a space $F(E)$ and a map $p: F(E) \to B$ such
	that the pullback $p^*(E) \to F(E)$ splits as a direct sum of line bundles, and
	\[
		p^*: H^*(B; \Z) \hookrightarrow H^*(F(E); \Z)
	\]
	is injective
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
	Consider the pullback $\P(\pi)^*(E)$ of $E$ via the map $\P(\pi):\P(E) \to B$ where $\P(E)$ is the space of
	lines through the origin in all fibers of $E$. This pullback contains a natural one-dimensional subbundle
	\[
		L = \set{(\ell,v) \in \P(E) \times E}{v \in \ell}
	\]
	An inner product on $E$ pulls back to an inner product on the pullback bundle, and thus a splitting of
	the pullback as a sum $L \oplus L^\perp$ with the orthogonal bundle $L^\perp$ having dimension one less
	than $E$. Then by the Leray-Hirsch theorem to $\P(E) \to B$, $H^*(\P(E);\Z)$
	is the free $H^*(B;\Z)$-module with basis $1,x,\ldots,x^{n-1}$ and in particular the induced map
	$H^*(B;\Z) \to H^*(\P(E);\Z)$ is injective since one of the basis elements is 1.

	This construction can be repeated with $L^\perp \to \P(E)$ in place of $E \to B$, until we get the
	splitting, completing the proof.
\end{Proof}

\section{Chern and Pontryagin Classes}

The axiomatic approach taken by Grothendeick shall be taken:

\begin{thm}{Existence and Uniqueness of Chern Classes}{existenceofChernClasses}
	Let $E \to X$ be a complex vector bundle. Then there exists a sequence of functions $c_1, c_2, ...$ where
	$c_i(E) \in H^{2i}(X; \Z)$ that depends only on the isomorphism type of $E$ such that
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $c_i(f^*(E) = f^*(c_i(E))$ for a pullback $f^*(E)$
		\item $c(E_1\oplus E_2) = c(E_1)\smile c(E_2)$ where $c = 1 + c_1 + c_2 + \cdots \in H^*(X;\Z)$
		\item $c_i(E) = 0$ if $i > \dim E$
		\item Given $E \to \CP^\infty$ is the canonical line bundle, $c_1(E)$ is a generator of
			$H^1(\CP^\infty; \Z)$ specified in advance.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  see~\cite{NateKTheory} for a full proof. The key idea is to invoke the Leray-Hirsch Theorem for existence
  and the splitting principle for uniqueness. In fact, we get a bit more out of the Leray-Hirsch theorem: by the classification of
  vector bundles (\cref{th:classifyingVecBundles}) there is map $f:E \to \CP^\infty$ which induces a
  cohomology pullback $f^*: H^*(\CP^\infty;\Z) \to H^*\left(\P(E);\Z\right)$, in particular $f^*:
  H^1(\CP^\infty;\Z) \to H^1(\P(E); \Z)$ which pullback the generator $\alpha \in H^1(\CP^\infty;\Z)$ to an
  element $f^*(\alpha) = x$. Then the elements $1, x, ..., x^n$ are the generators of $H^*(\P(E), \Z)$ as a
  free $H^*(X; \Z)$-module, and if $\alpha\beta :=\alpha\smile \beta$ then we get a unique relationship:
  \[
    x^n - c_1(E) + \cdots (-1)^n c_n(E)\cdot 1 = 0
  \]
  which gives a \emph{relationship} between the Chern classes.

  Uniqueness is given by the splitting principle, namely if here was another such relationship, then as the
  above equation must pullback and split, the Chern classes must match.
\end{Proof}

For the choice of normalization, note that for property (4), the canonical line bundle over $\CP^1$ could have
been used instead of $\CP^\infty$ as $\CP^1 \hookrightarrow \CP^\infty$ induces an isomorphism
\[
	H^1(\CP^\infty; \Z) \cong H^1(\CP^1;\Z)
\]
and we know by some cohomology theory that we can choose any hyperplane for the fundamental class of
$H^1(\CP^1;\Z)$, hence $c_1(E) \in H^1(\CP^1;\Z)$ becomes a choice of hyperplane.

Note that this definition differs slightly from what Hirzebruch presents: he replaces the third axiom with the
following:
\begin{center}
  \emph{(3) If $\xi_1, \ldots, \xi_q$ are continuous $\mathbf{U}(1)$-bundles over $X$ then}
\end{center}
\[
    c(\xi_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \xi_q) = c(\xi_1) \smile \cdots \smile c(\xi_q).
\]
The real equivalent has more subtly due to the fact that real manifolds and vector bundles have a choice of
orientation, and hence the natural cohomological setting to work in is over $\Z/2\Z$-coefficient and classify
$H^*(BO; \Z/2\Z)$ where we take $\Z/2\Z$ to get rid of choice of orientability. This uses
\emph{Stiefel-Whitney} classes which we did not touch on in this paper. To classify $H^*(BO; \Z)$, we
essentially take the ``complexification'' of a real vector bundle, which gives us the following best possible
result:

\index{Pontryagin Class}
\begin{defn}{Pontryagin Class}{pontryaginClass}
  Let $E \to B$ be a real vector bundle. Complexity it by adding the almost complex structure to $E\oplus E$:
  \[
    E^\C = E\oplus E \qquad i(x,y) = (-y,x)
  \]
  Then a \emph{Pontryagin class} $p_i(E) \in H^{4i}(B; \Z)$ is given by:
  \[
    p_i(E) = (-1)^ic_{2i}(E^\C) \in h^{4i}(B;\Z)
  \]
\end{defn}

To characterize Pontryagin classes, we state the following:

\begin{prop}{Characterizing Grassmannian Cohomology}{characterizingGrassmannianCohom}
    All torsion of $H^*(BO, \Z)$ is over $2$; if $T$ is the torsion subgroup then:
      \[
        \frac{H^*(BO, \Z)}{T} \cong \Z[p_1, ..., p_k] \qquad n = 2k\text{ or } 2k+1
      \]
\end{prop}

\begin{Proof}
  This is not important for our current work, so the proof is deferred to \cite{NateKTheory} or~\cite{hatcherVectorBundlesKTheory2017}
\end{Proof}

What shall be important to use is the use of Pontryagin classes in the theory of cobordism in

\begin{thm}{Chern and Pontryagin Relation}{chernPontryaginRelation}
  The Chern classes $c_i$ of the almost complex manifold $X$ are related to the Pontryagin
  classes $\cp_i$ of $X$ (regarded as a differentiable manifold) by:
\[
    \cp = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} (-1)^i \cp_i = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} c_i \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} (-1)^j c_j.
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}

\end{Proof}



\section{Chern Character and K-Theory}\label{sec:chernCharacter}

Fix a base space $X$ and let $K(X) = K_\C(X)$ be the set of isomorphism classes of complex vector bundles on
$X$. Let $c(-): K(X) \to H^*(X; \Z)$ be the Chern class. Recall that
\[
  c(E_1\oplus E_2) = c(E_1)\smile c(E_2)
\]
In particular, it brings the addition in $K(X)$ to multiplication in $H^*$. The collection $K(X)$ can be
completed to form a group, also denoted $K(X)$, and it is known as the \emph{Grothendieck K-group}. The tensor
gives it a natural ring structure. By the above construction, we see that $c(-)$ is \emph{not} a ring
homomorphism for it is not the case that $c(E\otimes F) = c(E) + c(F)$. From the Chern class $c(-)$, we can construct a new map known as the \emph{Chern character}
\[
  \ch(-): K(X) \to H^*(X; \Z)\otimes \Q
\]
which \emph{will} be a ring homomorphism, provided we add rational coefficients.

By the splitting principle, it suffices to work with line bundles. The total Chern class is:
\[
  c(L) = 1 + c_1(L)
\]
where $c_1(L) \in H^2(X; \Z)$. Then it is a worth-while exercise to show that for \emph{line
bundles}\footnote{use the correspondence between line bundles and cohomology classes,
see~\cite{NateKTheory}} (not for general vector bundles!):
\[
  c_1(L_1\otimes L_2) = c_1(L) + c_1(L)
\]
which as addition. To fix this into multiplication, we can take:
\[
  \ch(L) = e^{c_1(L)} = 1 + c_1(L) + \frac{c_1(L)^2}{2!} + \frac{c_1(L)^3}{3!} + \cdots
\]
where the power should be taken as repeated cup product. This fixes the multiplicative problem
for:
\[
\ch(L_1\otimes L_2) = e^{c_1(L_1) + c_1(L_2)} = e^{c_1(L_1)}\cdot e^{c_1(L_2)}
\]
But this introduces rational coefficients. As $H^*(X; \Z)$ can have torsion, ex. $H^*(\CP^1;\Z) \cong
\Z[h]/(h^2)$ (see~\cite[appendix]{NateAlgTop}), many of the terms here disappear and it is not well-defined.
Thus, we must define:
\[
  H^*(X; \Q) := H^*(X; \Z)\otimes \Q
\]
which in~\cite{NateAlgebra} it is shown that the torsion is eliminated, and hence $\ch(L) \in h^*(X; \Q)$ is
well-defined. Then, for an arbitrary vector bundle $E$, we can find a unique splitting
\[
  E = L_1\oplus L_2\oplus \cdots \oplus L_n
\]
and define:
\[
  \ch(E) = \ch(L_1) + \ch(L_2) + \cdots + \ch(L_n)
\]
where each $\ch(L_i) = t_i$ are the \emph{Chern root} of $c(E)$.  By construction:
\[
  \ch(E_1\oplus E_2) = \ch(E_1) + \ch(E_2)
\]
Giving the desired algebraic properties. However, $\ch(E)$ is dependent on the Chern classes $c_1(L_i)$
where $i$ goes over the Chern roots, as opposed to $c_j(E)$ where $j$ increased in dimension. This is
easily fixed with a bit of combinatorics. Namely, $c_j(E)$ is given by:
\[
  c_j(E) = \sigma_k(c_1(L_1), ..., c_1(L_n))
\]
where $\sigma_k$ is the $k$th elementary-symmetric polynomial. Define the \emph{power sums} to be:
\[
  s_k(x_1, ..., x_n) = \sum_i^n x_i^k
\]
Then the \emph{Newton identities} (see
\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_identities#Expressing_power_sums_in_terms_of_elementary_symmetric_polynomials}{this
link}) show that (via elementary computations):
\begin{align*}
  s_1 &= \sigma_1\\
  s_2 &= \sigma_1^2 - 2\sigma_2\\
  s_3 &= \sigma_1^3  -3\sigma_2\sigma_1  + 3 \sigma_3\\
  s_3 &= e_1^4 - 4e_2e_1^2 + 4e_3e_1 + 2e_2^2 - 4e_4\\
      &\vdots
\end{align*}

From these we get:
\[
\ch(E) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{t_i^k}{k!}\right) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\frac{1}{k!}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n s_k(c_1(E), ..., c_n(E))\right)
\]
Writing out the first few terms we get:
\[
  \ch(-) = \dim_\C(-) + c_1 + \frac{1}{2}(c_1^2 - 2c_2) + \frac{1}{6}(c_1^3 - 3c_1c_2 + 3c_3) + \cdots
\]
giving us a power series purely in terms of the Chern classes of $E$! Note that the Chern character in facts
captures more information than the Chern classes as the Chern classes are \emph{stable}, meaning they are
invariant when taking the direct sum with trivial bundles, however $\ch(-)$ has $\dim_\C(-)$ in its' $0$th
position, in particular it remembers the rank of the bundle\footnote{As a fun aside, if $X = \CP^n$, then the
Hilbert polynomial can be recovered by the Chern character, and vice-versa; see \cite{chanCorresondenceHilbertPolynomial2004}} To connect this to the $K$-group, we
require the following:

\begin{prop}{Extension and Tensors With Chern Character}{extandTensorsWithChernCharacter}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item let $E'', E, E'$ be vector bundles such that:
  \[
    0 \to E'' \to E \to E' \to 0
  \]
  then:
  \[
    \ch(E)(t) = \ch(E')(t) + \ch(E'')(t)
  \]
  And similarly
  \[
      \operatorname{ch}(E \otimes \eta)(t) = \sum_{i,j} e^{(\gamma_j+\delta_j)t} = \sum_{i,j} e^{\gamma_j t}e^{\delta_j t} = \left(\sum_i e^{\gamma_j t}\right)\left(\sum_j e^{\gamma_j t}\right) = \operatorname{ch}(E)(t)\operatorname{ch}(\eta)(t).
  \]
    \item Let $E_1, E_2$ be vector bundles. Then:
      \[
        \ch(E_1\otimes E_2) = \ch(E_1)\cdot \ch(E_2)
    \]
    where $\cdot$ is the cup product.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{Proof}
  exercise.
\end{Proof}

 Thus, there is a natural map:
\[
  \ch: K(X) \to H^*(X; \Q)
\]
This shows that $\ch(-)$ is the right notion for capturing the cohomological information of vector bundles which
respects the algebraic structure of $K(X)$. In chapter~\ref{cha:toddGenus}, we shall show that $\ch(E)$
capture the bundle information, while $\td(X)$ (define in chapter~\ref{cha:toddGenus}) shall capture the
relevant information from the underlying space.


A natural question to ask is whether $f: X \to Y$ is a continuous map, then does $\ch$ commutes with $f^*$, namely
if:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
K(Y) \arrow[r, "f^*"] \arrow[d, "\ch"'] & K(X) \arrow[d, "\ch"] \\
H^*(Y; \Q) \arrow[r, "f^*"]             & H^*(X; \Q)
\end{tikzcd}
\]
However this is not the case. What fixes the commutativity is the introduction of the Todd class. It is in
fact a little more complicated as we would usually be taking coherent sheaves and working with the
pushforward rather than the pullback, and so the details are left for section~\ref{sec:GRR}:

\[
\begin{tikzcd}
K(X) \arrow[r, "f_!"] \arrow[d, "\ch(-)\td(TX)"'] & K(Y) \arrow[d, "\ch(-)\td(TY)"] \\
A^*(X; \Q) \arrow[r, "f_*"]             & A^*(Y; \Q)
\end{tikzcd}
\]
where $A^*(-, \Q)$ is the \emph{chow ring} with rational coefficients.

\chapter{Cohomology of Varieties -- new Euler Characteristics}\label{cha:cohomVarieties}


The Euler characteristic is in a sense ``too rough'' of an invariant for varieties. We require to capture some
of the complex structure to find the right invariants. In particular, we shall refine the usual
De-Rham/singular cohomology into \emph{Dolbeault} cohomology which shall capture the obstruction to
a function/local section being \emph{holomorphic}. This shall form a new collection cohomology groups (depending on
the ``degree'') that will allow us to define new Euler-characteristics that generalize the topological Euler
Characteristic.

We start by developing some basic Hodge theory. The key point is to show that the Dolbeault cohomology groups

Then, as all smooth projective manifold can be embedded into $\CP^n$ have an algebraic equation by Chow's
theorem (\textcolor{red}{ref:HERE}), there is a natural compatibility between the Riemann metric, symplectic

defining what it means to be positive (essentially, insuring the tangent bundle is ``very ample'') we shall
show that all positive K\"ahler manifolds are a (quasi-projective) smooth variety. This will let us translate
the results over to algebraic geometry.

We then show how many interesting new Euler-characteristics can be
defined. We shall then end off by mentioning some important Vanishing theorems that would allow us to compute

Finally, we define the virtual Euler-characteristic which shall keep track of important intersection

\section{Dolbeault Cohomology}

We shall assume the basic knowledge of differential geometry that can be found in~\cite{NateDiffGeo}
or~\cite{leeIntroductionSmoothManifolds2003}, and of complex analysis that can be found
in~\cite{NateComplexAnalysis} or~\cite{ahlforsComplexAnalysisIntroduction2007}. This section shall be very
rapid to cover the key ideas necessary to define the Euler characteristics, sometimes omitting some proofs
that are better covered in a full text in Hodge Theory.

Let $X$ be a real manifold. Then we can take the fiber-wise tensor product $TM\otimes \C$ to complexity the
manifold. If $J: TM \to TM$ is an endomorphism st $J^2 = -1$, then $TM\otimes \C$ can be decmposed into:
\[
  TM\otimes \C T^{1,0}M \oplus T^{0,1}M
\]
where
\[
  T^{1,0}M = \set{x-iJx}{x \in TM} \qquad \text{and} \qquad T^{0,1}M =\set{x+iJx}{x \in TM}
\]
Thus, there is a natural decomposition of tangent bundles into holomorphic and anti-holomorphic components.
For a moment, let it be one-dimensional. As holomorphic mappings respects complex and anti-complex structure,
any 1-form with complex coefficients can be uniquely with respect written as a sum:
\[
   fdz + gd\overline{z}
\]
let $\Omega^{1,0}$ represent holomorphic $1$-forms, and $\Omega^{0,1}$ represent anti-holomorphic
$1$-forms.The spaces $\Omega^{1,0}$ and $\Omega^{0,1}$ are both stable under holomorphic coordinate
changes (use Cauchy-Riemann).

A similar pattern takes shape in higher dimensions. Let $X$ be $n$-dimensional. Like before, any $1$ form with
complex coefficients can be written as:
\[
  \sum_{j=1}^n (f_jdz^{j} + g_jd\overline{z}^j)
\]
Define:
\[
  \Omega^{p,q} = \underbrace{\Omega^{1,0} \wedge \cdots \wedge \Omega^{1,0}}_{\text{$p$ times}}\wedge \underbrace{\Omega^{0,1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \Omega^{0,1}}_{\text{$q$ times}}
\]
Then we know that for every $r \in \N$, every smooth $r$-form on $X$ (with $\C$-coefficients) can be written
as the sum of $(p,q)$-forms where $p+q = r$ (as the basis of these forms are wedge product of the lower
dimensions, see~\cite{NateDiffGeo}, \cite{NateAlgTop}).

Now, let $E^k$ be the space of all complex differential forms of total degree $k$. Then
\[
  E^k = \Omega^{k,0} \oplus \Omega^{k-1, 1}\oplus \cdots \oplus \Omega^{1, k-1}\oplus \Omega^{0,k}
  = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} \Omega^{p,q}
\]
if $M$ is is a complexified manifold, then:
\[
  \Omega^k(M, \C) = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} \Omega^{p,q}
\]
Note that the direct sum decomposition is stable under holomorphic coordinate changes, hence it determines
a vector bundle decomposition. From this direct product, there are natural projections for each direct summand:
\[
  \pi^{p,q}: E^K \to \Omega^{p,q}
\]
Now, define $d: \Omega^r \to \Omega^{r+1}$ in the natural way where:
\[
  d(\Omega^{p,q}) \sse \bigoplus_{r+s=p+q+1} \Omega^{r,s}
\]
\index{Dolbeault Operators}
\begin{defn}{Dolbeault Operators}{dolbeaultOperators}
  \[
    \partial = \pi_{p+1, q}\circ d \qquad \text{and} \qquad \overline{\partial} = \pi_{p, q+1}\circ d
  \]
\end{defn}
In coordinates, let:
\[
    \alpha = \sum_{|I|=p,|J|=q} f_{IJ} dz^I \wedge d\bar{z}^J \in \Omega^{p,q}
\]
where $I$ and $J$ are multi-indices. Then:
\[
    \partial\alpha = \sum_{|I|,|J|} \sum_\ell \frac{\partial f_{IJ}}{\partial z^\ell} dz^\ell \wedge dz^I \wedge d\bar{z}^J
\]
and
\[
    \bar{\partial}\alpha = \sum_{|I|,|J|} \sum_\ell \frac{\partial f_{IJ}}{\partial\bar{z}^\ell} d\bar{z}^\ell \wedge dz^I \wedge d\bar{z}^J.
\]
When $M$ is a complex manifold (equiv. it has a complex structure), then by the \emph{Newlander–Nirenberg
theorem}:
\[
    d = \partial + \bar{\partial}
\]
\[
    \partial^2 = \bar{\partial}^2 = \partial\bar{\partial} + \bar{\partial}\partial = 0.
\]
Thus, we see there is some interesting cohomology that can occur; this defines \emph{Dolbeault cohomology}.
The symbol $\partial$ suggests that they are dual to $d$, and indeed the Poincar\'e lemma generalizes to
complex manifolds.

Let us define the Dolbeault cohomology. Notice that $\overline{\partial}f = 0$ if $f$ is holomorphic,
therefore it is in some sense the ``natural'' choice; the other choice keeping track of the
``anti-holomorphic'' information. Thus, define:

\index{Dolbeault Cohomology}
\begin{defn}{Dolbeault Cohomology}{dolbeaultCohom}
  \[
H_{\overline{\partial}}^{p,q}(M) = \frac{\ker(\overline{\partial}: \Omega^{p,q} \to \Omega^{p,q+1})}{\text{im}(\overline{\partial}: \Omega^{p,q-1} \to \Omega^{p,q})}
\]
Were the $\overline{\partial}$ is dropped if it clear from context: $H^{p,q}(M)$.
\end{defn}

As
\[
  H_{\overline{\partial}}^{p,q}(M) = \overline{H_{\partial}^{p,q}(M)}
\]

We see that the difference of choice is a difference in conjugation, and hence we stick to
$\overline{\partial}$ for the holomorphic relation.

For our purposes, we will naturally want to generalize this to the Dolbeault cohomology of vector bundles. let
$E$ be a holomorphic vector bundle on a complex manifold $X$. Then take the map:
\[
  \overline{\partial}_E: \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^{0,1}(E)
\]
taking section of $E$ to $(0,1)$-forms with values in $E$. This will satisfy the Leibniz rule  with respect to
$\overline{\partial}$, and is thus a $(0,1)$-connection on $E$. Then we can do the same trick from Riemann
Geometry and extended it to  the exterior covariant derivative, namely;
\[
  \overline{\partial}_E: \Omega^{p,q}(E) \to \Omega^{p, q+1}(E)
\]
which acts on on a section $\alpha \otimes s \in \Omega^{p,q}(E)$ by:
\[
  \overline{\partial}_E(\alpha\otimes s) = (\overline{\partial}\alpha) \otimes s + (-1)^{p+q}\alpha \wedge
  \overline{\partial}_E s
\]
This naturally extends linearly to any section of $\Omega^{p,q}(E)$ and $\overline{\partial}_E^2 = 0$. Thus,
the Dolbeault cohomology with coefficient in $E$.
It is standard to check that the Dolbeault cohomology groups do not depend on the choice of Dolbeault operator
$\overline{\partial}_E$ compatible with the holomorphic structure of $E$
\cite{gallierComplexAlgebraicGeometry2011}, and so:
\index{Dolbeault Cohomology For Vector Bundles}
\begin{defn}{Dolbeault Cohomology For Vector Bundles}{dolbeaultCohomForVecBundles}
  \[
H^{p,q}(X,(E,\bar{\partial}_E)) = \frac{\ker(\bar{\partial}_E : \Omega^{p,q}(E) \to \Omega^{p,q+1}(E))}{\im(\bar{\partial}_E : \Omega^{p,q-1}(E) \to \Omega^{p,q}(E))}
\]
which is unambiguously denoted as:
\[
  H^{p,q}(X,E)
\]
\end{defn}
if $\mathbbm{1}$ is the trivial bundle, then we get back the usual Dolbeault cohomology.

Let $\Omega^p$ be the sheaf of holomorphic $p$-forms on $M$. Then the following is the natural
generization of DeRham's theorem for Dolbeault cohomology:
\begin{thm}{Dolbeault's Theorem}{dolbeaultThm}
  \[
  H^{p,q}(M) \cong H^q(M, \Omega^p)
\]
and if we are working over a vector bundle:
\[
  H^{p,q}(M, E) \cong H^q(M, \Omega^p\otimes E)
\]
Furthermore:
\[
  H^{0,q}(M, E) \cong H^q(M, \Omega^0\otimes E) = H^q(M, E)
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  This proof is very similar to the DeRham case. However, it can also be proven by taking a resolution of
  $\Omega^{p,q}$ using \emph{fine sheaves} (sheaves that admit partitions of unity)\footnote{flasque sheaves are
  not fine, but are the next best thing they are \emph{soft}: every section in a closed subset can be extended
  to the whole sheaf} and applying the $\overline{\partial}$-lemma
  (see~\cite{gallierComplexAlgebraicGeometry2011} or~\cite[section~15.4]{hirzebruchTopologicalMethodsAlgebraic1995}).
\end{Proof}

For future reference, if $E$ is a complex vector bundle, we shall denote its conjugate by $\overline{E}$
(defined in the natural way) which is anti-complex and anti-isomorphic to $E$. We can also define the dual
bundle $E^*$ in the natural way. Note that per-fiber, the dual can be given by Hermitian of the inverse of the
matrix.

\subsection*{Hodge Theorem: Finiteness of Cohomology Groups}

Let $M$ now be compact and complex. Let us build-up to showing that each class in $H^{p,q}(M, \C)$ is
representable by a harmonic form. This will allow us to quote result from the theory of elliptic operators
that will show these vector spaces are finite dimensional. For
notational simplicity, let:
\[
  A^{p,q}(W) = \Gamma(V, \Omega^{p,q}(W))
\]
and:
\[
  A^{p,q} = A^{p,q}(\mathbbm{1})
\]
where $\mathbbm{1}$ is the trivial bundle representing holomorphic functions. We shall define the Hodge
operator. If $W$ is a complex vector bundle, we can define $W^*$ and a (perfect) pairing:
\[
  A^{p,q}(W) \times A^{r,s}(W^*) \to A^{p+r, q+s}(\mathbbm{1}) \qquad (\alpha,\beta) \mapsto \alpha\wedge
\beta
\]
If $W = \mathbbm{1}$, this is the usual wedge (or exterior) product. The product is anti-commutative in the
sense that:
\[
  \alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{(p+r)(q+s)} \beta \wedge  \alpha
\]
Choosing $r = n-p$, $s = n-q$ we get the top form $\alpha \wedge  \beta \in A^{n,n}(\mathbbm{1})$. We can
thus define:
\[
  \langle \alpha,\beta \rangle = \int_M \alpha \wedge \beta
\]
By Stokes theorem, if $\alpha = \overline{\partial}\gamma$ for $\gamma \in A^{p, q-1}(W)$ and
$\overline{\partial}\beta = 0$, (or if $\overline{\partial}\alpha = 0$ and $\beta
= \overline{\partial}\gamma$) then:
\[
  \langle \alpha,\beta \rangle = \int_M \alpha \wedge \beta = \int_M \overline{\partial}(\gamma \wedge \beta)
  = \int_M d \left(\gamma \wedge  \beta\right) = 0
\]
Thus, we get a well-defined pairing:
\[
  H^{p,q}(M,E) \times H^{n-q, n-q}(M, E^*) \to \C\qquad (\alpha, \beta) \mapsto \langle \alpha,\beta \rangle
  = \int_M \alpha \wedge  \beta
\]
Now, by some linear algebra and differential geometry we see that $\overline{TM} \cong T^*M$, and so:
\begin{align*}
\bigwedge^p TM \otimes \bigwedge^q TM &\cong \bigwedge^p TM \otimes \bigwedge^n T^*M \otimes \bigwedge^n TM \otimes \bigwedge^q T^*M \\
&\cong \bigwedge^{n-p} T^*M \otimes \bigwedge^{n-q} TM \\
&\cong \bigwedge^{n-q} TM \otimes \bigwedge^{n-p} \overline{TM}
\end{align*}
There is thus a natural duality operator:
\[
  *: \bigwedge^p TM \otimes \bigwedge^q TM \to \bigwedge^{n-q} TM \otimes \bigwedge^{n-p} \overline{TM}
\]
and $*\circ *$ becomes multiplication by $(-1)^{p+q}$.

Now, by vector-bundle theory we know we can without loss of generality reduce $W$ to a unitary group bundle.
There is then the natural hermitian anti-isomorphism:
\[
  \psi: W \to W^* \qquad \psi^{-1}: W^* \to W
\]
If $\kappa$ is the anti-isomorphism from $\bigwedge^r TM \otimes \overline{\bigwedge^s TM}$ to itself via
conjugation, Then define:
\[
  \star = \psi \otimes (\kappa \circ *) \qquad \widetilde{\star} = \psi^{-1} \otimes (\kappa \circ *)
\]
This gives:
\begin{align*}
\star &: W \otimes \bigwedge^p TM \otimes \overline{\bigwedge^q TM} \to W^* \otimes \bigwedge^{n-p} TM \otimes \overline{\bigwedge^{n-q} TM} \\
\widetilde{\star} &: W^* \otimes \bigwedge^r TM \otimes \overline{\bigwedge^s TM} \to W \otimes \bigwedge^{n-r} TM \otimes \overline{\bigwedge^{n-s} TM}.
\end{align*}

If $r = n-p$, $s = n-q$, then $\widetilde{\star}\circ \star$ is multiplication by $(-1)^{p+q}$. Now, these
produce natural anti-isomorphisms of:
\begin{align*}
  \star&: \Omega^{p,q}(W) \to \Omega^{n-p, n-q}(W^*)\\
  \widetilde{\star}&: \Omega^{r,s}(W^*) \to \Omega^{n-r, n-s}(W)\\
\end{align*}

Using these, we can define the ``co-'' of $\overline{\partial}$:
\[
  \delta: \Omega^{p,q}(W) \to \Omega^{p, q-1}(W) \qquad \delta= -\widetilde{\star} \circ
  \overline{\partial} \circ \star
\]
With this, if $\alpha,\beta \in A^{p,q}(W)$ then we can define:
\[
  \left( \alpha,\beta \right) = \langle \alpha, \star \beta \rangle = \int_M \alpha \wedge  \star \beta
\]
It is easy to see that $\left( \alpha,\alpha \right) \ge 0$ and $\left(\alpha,\alpha  \right) = 0$ if and only
if $\alpha = 0$. We also get the natural adjoint:
\[
  \left(\alpha, \delta \beta\right) = \langle \overline{\partial}\alpha, \beta \rangle
\]
Indeed given:
\[
  \langle\alpha, \delta \beta\rangle = - \int_M \alpha \wedge  \star \widetilde{\star}\overline{\partial} \beta = (-1)^{p+q+1} \int_M \alpha \wedge \overline{\partial} \star \beta
\]
Then:
\begin{align*}
    (\overline{\partial}\alpha, \beta) - (\alpha, \delta \beta) &= \int_M (\overline{\partial}\alpha \wedge \star \beta + (-1)^{p+q} \alpha \wedge \overline{\partial} \star \beta) \\
    &= \int_M \overline{\partial}(\alpha \wedge \star \beta) \\
    &= \int_M d(\alpha \wedge \star \beta) \\
    &= 0 & \text{Stokes Thm.}
\end{align*}


We now reach one of the central results in Hodge theory. Define the \emph{Hodge operator} (or Laplace-Bertram
operator):
\[
  \Delta = \delta\overline{\partial} + \overline{\partial}\delta
\]
This defines an operator:
\[
  \Delta: A^{p,q}(W) \to A^{p,q}(W)
\]
Let
\[
  \ker\Delta = B^{p,q}(W)
\]
which could be interpreted as all ``complex harmonic forms'', namely forms that satisfy:
\[
  \delta(\alpha) = \overline{\partial}(\alpha) = 0
\]
The use of the elliptic operator is that it gives way to the following decomposition:
\[
  A^{p,q}(W) = \overline{\partial}A^{p, q-1}(W) \oplus \delta A^{p, q+1}(W) \oplus B^{p,q}(V,W)
\]
Notice that we naturally get:
\[
  Z^{p,q}(W) = \overline{\partial}A^{p, q-1}(W) \oplus B^{p,q}(V,W)
\]
Thus, when taking the Dolbeault cohomology group:
\[
  H^{p,q}(V,W) \cong \frac{Z^{p,q}(W)}{\overline{\partial}A^{p,q}(W)} \cong B^{p,q}(V,W)
\]
Hence, the forms are naturally harmonic! This result is called the \emph{Hodge Theorem}. Furthermore, as this
is an elliptic operator, we get by elliptic operator theory that each of these spaces is \emph{finite
dimensional} (see~\cite{HodgeTheory2014}). We thus also get:
\[
  H^r(M, \C) \cong \bigoplus_{p+q=r} B^{p,q}
\]
and the Betti number is the sum of Hodge numbers:
\[
  b_r(V) = \sum_{p+q=r} h^{p,q}(M)
\]
For future reference, an element of $H^{p+q}(M, \Z)$ or  $H^{p+q}(M, \R)$ is said to be of type $(p,q)$ if it
is of type $(p,q)$ when regarded as an element of $H^{p+q}(M, \C)$.

All of this is summarized in the following two theorems:

\begin{thm}{Kodaira Finite Dimension Theorem}{kodariaFinDimThm}
   The Dolbeault cohomology group $H^{p,q}(M,E)$ is finite dimensional
  isomorphic to the vector space of complex harmonic forms of type $(p,q)$ with coefficients in $E$.
  Furthermore:
  \[
    H^p(M,E) = H^{0,p}(M,E)
  \]
  and is zero for $p,q > n$.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  See for example~\cite{HodgeTheory2014} or~\cite{voisinHodgeTheoryComplex2002}.
\end{Proof}

\begin{thm}{Serre-Hodge Duality Theorem}{serreHodgeDuality}
  Let $E$ be a complex vector bundle over the compact manifold $M$. Then the bilinear form $\langle -,-
  \rangle$ is a dual pairing of $H^{p,q}(M,E)$ with $H^{n-p, n-q}(M, E^*)$. If $K = \bigwedge^n TM$ is the
  canonical line bundle, Then $H^p(M,E)$ and $H^{n-q}(M, K\otimes E^*)$ are dual vector spaces.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  See for example~\cite{HodgeTheory2014} or~\cite{voisinHodgeTheoryComplex2002}.
\end{Proof}

\index{Hodge Number}
\begin{defn}{Hodge Number}{hodgeNumber}
  \[
    h^{p,q}(M,E) = \dim H^{p,q}(M,E)
  \]
  If $E = \mathbbm{1}$ then:
  \[
    h^{p,q}(M) = \dim H^{p,q}(M,\mathbbm{1})
  \]
\end{defn}

Note that in general $h^{p,q}(M) \ne h^{q,p}(M)$; if $M$ is a K\"ahler manifold this will be the case.


\section{Geometric-Algebraic Bridge: K\"ahler Manifold}\label{sec:kahlerMan}

Let $M$ be a compact complex Riemann manifold. A Hermitian metric on $M$ is defined as:
\[
  ds^2 = 2 \sum g_{\alpha\beta}(z, \overline{z}) (dz^\alpha \cdot d \overline{z}^\beta)
\]
where $\overline{g_{\alpha\beta}} = g_{\beta\alpha}$. From a Hermitian metric $ds^2$, we get a natural exterior
differential form:
\[
  \omega = i\sum g_{\alpha\beta}(z, \overline{z}) dz^\alpha \wedge  d \overline{z}^\beta
\]
This form can be re-written as a real differential form by taking real coordinates $x^\alpha$, $1 \le \alpha
\le 2n$ and
\[
  z^\alpha = x^{2\alpha-1} + ix^{2\alpha}
\]
\index{K\"ahler Metric}
\begin{defn}{K\"ahler Metric}{kahlerMetric}
  Let $ds^2$ be a Hermitian metric on the compact complex Riemann manifold $M$ and let:
  \[
    \omega = i\sum g_{\alpha\beta}(z, \overline{z}) dz^\alpha \wedge  d \overline{z}^\beta
  \]
  Then if $d\omega = 0$, $ds^2$ is called a \emph{K\"ahler metric}.
\end{defn}

If $ds^2$ is a K\"ahler metric, $\omega$ represents an element of $H^2(M, \R)$ (using the De-Rham
isomorphism); this is called the \emph{fundamental class of the K\"ahler metric}.

\index{K\"ahler Manifold}
\begin{defn}{K\"ahler Manifold}{kahlerManifold}
  Let $M$ be a compact complex Riemann manifold with Riemann metric $ds^2$. Then given a choice of $\omega$ that
  induces $ds^2$ to be K\"ahler metric, $(M, \omega)$ is called a \emph{K\"ahler manifold}.
\end{defn}

On a K\"ahler manifold, $\Delta$ commutes with the conjugation $\alpha \mapsto \overline{\alpha}$ between
$B^{q,p}$ and $B^{p,q}$ which induces an isomorphism between these groups, and so:
\begin{thm}{Symmetry of Hodge Number On K\"ahler Manifold}{symmetryofHodgeNumberOnKahlerManifold}
  Let $M$ be a K\"ahler manifold. Then:
  \[
  h^{p,q}(M) = h^{q,p}(M)
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  See for example~\cite{HodgeTheory2014} or~\cite{voisinHodgeTheoryComplex2002}.
\end{Proof}

Note that these numbers do not depend on the choice of K\"ahler metric ($d\alpha = 0$ for a harmonic
$(p,q)$-form regardless of metric) and hence it is enough for a K\"ahler metric to exist.

Now, being a K\"ahler manifold is very close to being a projective variety. Recall from Hartshorne that
a sheaf on a scheme is very ample\footnote{or ample, meaning it is very ample after tensoring with an
appropriate $\CO(n)$} if has ``enough projectives'' (see Hartshorne's
exposition at~\cite{hartshorneAlgebraicGeometry2013} or my exposition at~\cite{NateAlgGeo}). Not all K\"ahler
manifolds have enough projectives, but it is a surprisingly simple condition that insures that a K\"ahler
manifold can embed into projective space.

\index{Positivity!Real}
\begin{defn}{Real Positivity}{realpositivity}
  Let $x \in H^{1,1}(M, \R)$. Then $x$ is said to be \emph{positive} if $x$ can be choasen as the fundamental
  class of a  metric on $M$. K\"ahler
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}{Properties of Real Positivity}{propofRealPositivity}
  Let $M$ be a K\"ahler manifold. Then
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item At least one element of  $H^{1,1}(M,\R)$ is positive
    \item The zero element of $H^{1,1}(M,\R)$ is not positive
    \item If $x >0$ and $y > 0$ then $x+y> 0$ ($x,y \in H^{1,1}(M, \R)$)
    \item If $x >0$ and $r > 0$ then $rx> 0$ ($x \in H^{1,1}(M, \R)$, $r \in \R$)
    \item if $x,y \in H^{1,1}(M, \R)$ and $x > 0$, then there exists a posotivei integer $g$ (depending on
      $x,y$) such that $gx-y > 0$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{Proof}
  exercise.
\end{Proof}

\index{Positivity!Integral}
\begin{defn}{Integral Positivity}{intPositivity}
  Let $M$ be a K\"ahler manifold and $x \in H^{1,1}(M, \Z)$. Then $x$ is said to be \emph{positive} if it is
  positive when regarded as an element of $H^{1,1}(M, \R)$.
\end{defn}

\index{Hodge Manifold}
\begin{defn}{Hodge Manifold}{hodgeManifold}
  A K\"ahler manifold $M$ is called a \emph{Hodge Manifold} if $H^{1,1}(M, \Z)$ contains at least one positive
  element.
\end{defn}

Another way of saying this is that $M$ admits a K\"ahler metric whose fundamental class is in the image of the
natural homomorphism $H^2(M, \Z) \to H^2(M, \R)$.

\begin{example}{Complex Space is Hodge Manifold}{complexSpisHodgeManifold}
  let $M = \CP^n$. Then
  \[
    H^{1,1}(\CP^n, \Z) = H^2(\CP^n, \Z) \cong \Z
  \]
  and so if $x \in H^{1,1}(\CP^n, \R) = H^2(\CP^n, \R)$, there is a real number $r > 0$ such that $rx$ lies in
  the image of the aforementioned inclusion homomorphism. In particular, the positive elements of $H^2(\CP^n,
  \Z)$ are the integral multiples of the cohomology classes of oriented planes.
\end{example}

\begin{thm}{Hodge Manifold Are Algebraic}{hodgeManifoldAreAlgebraic}
  Let $M$ be a K\"ahler manifold. Then $M$ embeds into $\CP^n$ for some $n$ if and only if it is a Hodge
  Manifold.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  see~\cite{voisinHodgeTheoryComplex2002}.
\end{Proof}

Similarly, of $L$ is a line bundle over $M$, then it is \emph{projectively induced} if for some embedding of
$M$ into $\CP^n$, $L$ is the restriction of $M$ to a line bundle $H$ with cohomology class $h_n$ ($h_n$
representing the cohomology class of characterized by a hyperplane). Mirroring the result in divisor theory,
a projectively induced line bundle can always be given by a divisor:


\begin{thm}{Bertini Theorem}{bertiniThm}
  Let $F$ be a projectively induced line bundle over the algebraic manifold $M$. Then a divisor
  $D$ induces $F$, namely $F = \CL(D)$.
\end{thm}

This is actually a special case of the general Bertini theorem that you find when googling ``Bertini
Theorem''.

\begin{Proof}
  \cite{voisinHodgeTheoryComplex2002}
\end{Proof}

Next, just like in sheaf cohomology over schemes, higher-order cohomology shall vanish (given the vectr bundle
is sufficiently ``twisted'' to be very ample):

\begin{thm}{Kodaira Higher Order Vanishing Theorem}{kodairaVanishingThmII}
  Let $F$ be a complex line bundle over a K\"ahler manifold $M$, and let $F'$ be a positive line bundle over $M$.
  Then
  \[
    H^i(M, F\otimes (F')^k)
  \]
  vanish for all $i > 0$ and $k$ sufficiently large.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  \cite{voisinHodgeTheoryComplex2002}
\end{Proof}

From it we get:

\begin{thm}{Characterizing Line Bundles On Algebraic Manifold}{characterizingLineBundlesOnAlgebraicManifold}
  Let $M$ be an Hodge Manifold, $F$ an analytic line bundle over $M$. Then there are projectively induced
  line bundles $A,B$ where $F = A\otimes B^{-1}$. Thus, we have:
  \[
    F = \CL(A)\otimes \CL(B)^{-1}
  \]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  see~\cite[p.141, thm~18.2.5]{hirzebruchTopologicalMethodsAlgebraic1995}.
\end{Proof}

\section{Euler Characteristic and Generalization}\label{sec:eulerCharAndGeneralization}

In the final proof, we shall work with smooth projective varieties and locally free coherent sheaves, but it
will do not harm at the start of this section to let $M$ be a smooth compact manifold and $E$ a complex vector
bundle; we shall re-introduce more assumptions as needed. As we have a double complex, we can define more than
one Euler characteristic. First, recall there is the natural:
\[
  \chi(M,E) = \sum_{i=0}^\infty \dim H^i(M,E) = \sum_{i=0}^n \dim H^i(M,E)
\]
If $E = \mathbbm{1}$, we shall denote it as $\chi_\Top$ and call it the \emph{Topological Euler
characteristic}. By~\cref{th:kodariaFinDimThm} and~\cref{th:serreHodgeDuality} there is a natural grading so
that we can define:
\[
  \chi^p(M,E) = \chi\left(V, E\otimes \bigwedge^p TM\right) = \sum_{q=0}^n (-1)^q h^{p,q}(M,E)
\]
Immediately we get:
\[
  \chi^0(M,E) = \chi(M,E) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \chi^p(M,E) = 0\text{ when $p < 0$ or $p > n$}
\]
and if $E = \mathbbm{1}$ then:
\[
  \chi^p(M,\mathbbm{1}) = \chi^p(M) = \sum_{q=0}^n (-1)^q h^{p,q}(M)
\]
We can collect these characteristic in a generating function:
\index{Euler Characteristic!Euler-Hodge}
\begin{defn}{$\chi_y$-characteristic}{totalEulerCharacteristic}
    \[
    \chi_y(M,E) = \sum_{p=0}^n \chi^p(M,E)y^p \qquad \text{(sim.)} \qquad \chi_y(M) = \sum_{p=0}^n \chi^p(M)y^p
  \]
  The term $\chi_y(M,E)$ is the $\chi_y$-characteristic of $E$ and $\chi_y(M)$ is called the $\chi_y$-genus of
  $M$
\end{defn}

By construction:
\[
  \chi_0(M,E) = \chi^0(M,E) = \chi(M,E) \qquad \text{(sim.)} \qquad \chi_0(M) = \chi^0(M) = \chi(M)
\]
Inspired by Hartshorne~\cite{hartshorneAlgebraicGeometry2013}, call:
% As the Euler-Hodge characteristic was called a genus, we shall call:
\[
  \chi(M) = \sum_{q=0}^n (-1)^q h^{0,q}(M)
\]
the \emph{arithmetic genus} of $M$. By Serre Duality:
\begin{align*}
  \chi^p(M,E) &= (-1)^n \chi^{n-p}(M, E^*)\\
  \chi(M,E)&= (-1)^n \chi(M, K\otimes E^*)
\end{align*}


Let us now assume $M$ is K\"ahler. Let $g_q = h^{q,0}$. Then by the symmetry, the arithmetic genus $\chi(M)$
of a compact K\"ahler manifold is equal to:
\[
  \chi(M) = \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^ig_i
\]
If $M$ and $N$ are K\"ahler manifolds then by K\"unneths formula:
\[
    h^{p,q}(M \times N) = \sum_{\substack{r+u=p \\ s+v=q}} h^{r,s}(M) h^{u,v}(N).
\]

Let $y$, $z$ be indeterminates and associate to each K\"ahler manifold $V$ the polynomial $\prod_{y,z}(V)
= \sum_{p,q} h^{p,q} y^p z^q$. Then the above is equivalent to:
\[
    \prod_{y,z}(M \times N) = \prod_{y,z}(M) \cdot \prod_{y,z}(N).
\]

Let $z = -1$ in. Then $\prod_{y,-1}(-) = \chi_y(-)$ an so:
\[
    \chi_y(M \times N) = \chi_y(M) \cdot \chi_y(N),
\]
Next, let us understand $\chi_y(M)$ for specific values of $y$. Let us start with $y = -1$. Then:
\[
  \chi_{-1}(M) = \sum_{p=0}^n (-1)^p\chi^p(M) = \sum_{p,q} (-1)^{p+q} h^{p,q}(M)
\]
is equal to the usual Euler characteristic.

The most important value to prove will be for when $y = 1$. In this case, the $\chi_{1}$-characteristic will
be equal to a value called the \emph{(spin) index} of $M$. When defining defining a generating function $T_y$ that
will hold the Chern character and Todd class information, $y = -1$ shall represent $\ch(E)\smile \td(M)$, and when $y = 1$ it shall \emph{also} be equal to the
index of $M$, and the proof of the Hirzebruch Riemann Roch boils down to showing that this forces the
coefficients of $T_y$ and $\chi_y$ to be the equal and hence they must also agree when $y = -1$. The index results
shall be proven in chapter~\ref{cha:cobordism} and the specific theorem for the character when $y=1$ is proven
in~\cref{th:eulerCharandIndex}.




Let us finish with the special case $M$ a K\"ahler manifolds and $h^{p,q} = 0$ when $p\ne q$. Then $\chi_y(M)$
is (essentially) equal to the Poincar\'e polynomial:
\[
  P(t; M) = \sum b_rt^r
\]
where $b_r$ is the $r$th Betti number of $M$. Indeed, we get:
\[
  \chi^p(M) = \sum_q (-1)^1 h^{p,q} = (-1)^p h^{p,p} = (-1)b_{2p}
\]
therefore:
\[
  \chi_{-t^2}(M) = P(t;V) = \sum_r b_rt^r
\]
Why do we care about this special case? Because complex projectives varieties $\CP^n$ and flag manifolds
$F(n)$ both are K\"ahler manifolds with this property. The $\CP^n$ comes from basic Dolbeault cohomology. For
the flag manifold $F(n)$, recall that $H^*(F(n), \Z)$ by the splitting principle is generated by element
$\gamma_i \in H^2(F(n); \Z)$. Then by characteristic theory there are complex $\C^*$-bundles $\xi_i$ over
$F(n)$ where $c_1(\xi_i) = \gamma_i$. The ``only if'' comes from~\cref{th:charLineBundleKahlMan} where we have
that each $\gamma_i$ is of type $(1,1)$, and therefore the cohomology classes of $F(n)$ are of type $(p,p)$.
Let us build-up to prove this theorem.

Take the usual exact sequence:
\[
  0 \to \underline{\Z} \to \CO_M \to \CO_M^* \to 0
\]
where $\underline{\Z}$ is the constant sheaf, $\CO_M$ is the sheaf of holomorphic functions, and $\CO_M^*$ is
the sheaf of never-zero holomorphic functions (invertible holomorphic functions). Then we get an exact
cohomology sequence:
\[
  H^1(M, \CO_M^*) \xrightarrow{\delta_*^1} H^2(M, \Z) \to H^2(M, \CO_M^*)
\]
Then on a K\"ahler manifold we have $H^2(M, \CO_X^*) = H^2(M, \mathbbm{1}) \cong B^{0,2}(M)$. Thus, an element
$a \in H^2(M, \Z)$ is mapped to a nonzero element of $H^2(M, \CO_M)$ if and only if $a$ is of type $(1,1)$.
Then from characteristic theory for vector bundles we know that if $\xi \in H^1(M, \CO_M^*)$ is a complex
$\C^*$-bundle, then:
\[
  \delta_*^1(\xi) = c_1(\xi)
\]
If $F$ is a complex line bundle over $M$ and $\xi$ is the assocaited $\C^*$-bundle, then $c_1(\xi)$ is the
\emph{cohomology class} of $F$. Then we get:

\begin{thm}{characterising Line bundles on K\"ahler Manifolds}{charLineBundleKahlMan}
  Let $M$ be a compact K\"ahler manifold. Then an element $a \in H^2(M, \Z)$ is the cohomology class of
  a complex line bundle over $M$ if and only if it is of type $(1,1)$.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  Hirzebruch references ppl at~\cite[p.127, Thm~15.9.1]{hirzebruchTopologicalMethodsAlgebraic1995}, and
  says it is provable for non-K\"ahler manifolds and was done by Dolbeault.
\end{Proof}

\subsection{Euler Characteristic on Intersections}\label{sec:propGenerlizedEulerChar}

The Hirzebruch Riemann Roch will reduce to being proven on line bundles. As line bundles on projective
varieties are associated to codimension $1$ subschemes, we can take a filtering of $M$ of codimension $1$
subschemes to represent the line bundles. This will lead to decomposing $\chi$ in terms of the characteristic
of all possible filterings. To that end, we shall develop some more properties of $\chi_y$. Let
:
\[
  0 \to E' \to E \to E'' \to 0
\]
be a short exact sequence of complex vector bundles. This naturally induces a short exact sequence of
sheaves~\cite{NateAlgGeo}:
\[
  0 \to \CO_{E'} \to \CO_E \to \CO_{E''} \to 0
\]
which we take as we can take advantage of some sheaf theory in the following proofs. What is important is that
we get that we get that each new Euler-characteristic is additive on exact sequences:
\begin{thm}{Generalized Euler Characteristic Additive}{generalizedEulerCharAdditive}
  Let
\[
  0 \to \CO_{E'} \to \CO_E \to \CO_{E''} \to 0
\]
  be a short exact sequence of complex vector bundles over $M$. Then:
  \begin{align*}
   \chi(M, E) &= \chi(M, E') + \chi(M, E'')\\
   \chi^p(M, E) &= \chi^p(M, E') + \chi^p(M, E'')\\
   \chi_y(M, E) &= \chi_y(M, E') + \chi_y(M, E'')
  \end{align*}
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  exercise (also an exercise in Hartshorne, see~\cite[section~2.5]{hartshorneAlgebraicGeometry2013})
\end{Proof}

\begin{thm}{Splitting Principle and Euler Characteristic}{splittingPrincipleandEulerChar}
  Let  $E$ be a complex vector bundle over a compact complex manifold $M$, and suppose the structure group of
  $E$ can be complex analytically reduced to  the triangular group $\Delta(q, \C)$. Let $A_1, A_2, ..., A_q$
  be the corresponding diagonal line bundles. Let $E'$ be another complex vector bundle over $M$. Then:
  \[
    \chi(M, E'\otimes E) = \sum_i^q \chi(M, E'\otimes A_i)
  \]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  Use induction and the above theorem
\end{Proof}

Thus, $\chi$ on vector bundles split additivity, similarly to how the Chern character $\ch$ behaves.

As Cartier divisors are in correspondence to line bundles, let us see how they come into the picture. As usual
let $E$ be a complex vector bundle over a complex manifold $M$, and let $D$ be a  divisor of $M$.
Then it is associate to a line bundle $\CO_M(D) = \CL(D)$. We can now tensor $E\otimes \CL(D)$, and consider
$(E\otimes \CL(D))_D$ to be the restriction to $D$ of the vector bundle $E\otimes \CL(D)$. We can take the
sheaf $\CO((E\otimes \CL(D))_D)$ over $D$. By Extending by zero, this is a sheaf on $M$ (in Hartshorne, this
is denoted by the pushforward on the inclusion map $j_*(D)$~\cite{hartshorneAlgebraicGeometry2013}, where
$j_*$ can also be interpreted as the Gysin map as described by Fulton~\cite{fultonIntersectionTheory1984}). By
the corresponding of vector bundles and sheaf, we may without loss of generality denote this sheaf by
$\CO_{E}|_D$.

\begin{thm}{Extending via Divisors}{extViaDivisors}
  let $M$ be a complex manifold , $D$ a  Cartier divisor of $M$, $E$ a complex vector bundle over
  $M$. Then there is a short exact sequence:
  \[
    0 \to \CO_E \to \CO_{E\otimes \CL(D)} \to \CO_{E}|_D \to 0
  \]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  This is a standard proof, this can be found in pure-sheaf form in
  Hartshorne~\cite[chapter~2]{hartshorneAlgebraicGeometry2013} or EYTNKA alg geo~\cite{NateAlgGeo}.
\end{Proof}

% Let $M$ now be compact and $D$ still a  divisor. It now is always a compact manifold.
When it is not confusing, we shall denote $E_D$ to be the complex vector bundle over $D$. We shall also write:
\begin{align*}
  \chi(D, E) &:= \chi(D, E_D)\\
  \chi^p(D, E) &:= \chi^p(D, E_D)\\
  \chi_y(D, E) &:= \chi_y(D, E_D)
\end{align*}
\begin{thm}{Splitting Euler Characteristic Using Divisor}{splitPrinEulerCharAndDivisor}
  Let $M$ be a compact complex manifold, $D$ a  divisor, $E$ a complex vector bundle over $M$. Then:
  \[
    \chi(M,E) = \chi(M, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1}) + \chi(D, E)
  \]
  In particular, if $E = \mathbbm{1}$, then:
  \[
    \chi(M) = \chi(M, \CL(D)^{-1}) + \chi(D)
  \]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  combine above results
\end{Proof}

We want to extend this inductively so that $\chi_y$ can be written in terms of $\chi^p$. First, recall from
Riemann Geometry that we get the following short exact sequence:
\[
  0 \to T(D) \to T(M)|_D \to N_{M/D} \to 0
\]
where $N_{M/D}$ is the normal bundle of $D$ with respect to $M$. It is not hard to see it's fibers are of
dimension $1$, and a little more work shows it is isomorphic to $\CL(D)_D$, and so:
\[
  0 \to T(D) \to T(M)|_D \to \CL(D)_D \to 0
\]
We then get a natural exact sequence:
\[
0 \to \bigwedge^p\left(T(D)\right) \to \bigwedge^p(T(M)|_D) \to \bigwedge^{p-1}(T(D)) \otimes \CL(D)_D\to 0
\]
Dualizing we get:
\[
  0 \to \bigwedge^{p-1}(T^*(D))\otimes \CL(D)_D^{-1} \to \bigwedge^p(T^*(M)|_D) \to \bigwedge^p(T^*(D)) \to 0
\]
Now if $E$ is a complex vector bundle over $M$, we can tensor each term by $E_D$. Then by the above theorems:
\[
  \chi\left(D, E\otimes \bigwedge(T^*(M))\right) = \chi^{p-1}(D, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1}) + \chi^p(D, E)
\]
Replacing $E$ with $E\otimes \bigwedge^p(T^*(M))$, manipulating we get:
\index{Four-term Formula}
\begin{defn}{Four-term Formula}{four-termFormula}
  \[
  \chi^p(M,E) = \chi^p(M, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1}) + \chi^p(D, E) + \chi^{p-1}(D, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1})
  \]
\end{defn}
This is true for $p>0$, and for $p=0$ interpret the last term on the right hand side as being $0$.
Furthermore, $\chi^p(D, E)=0$ for $p = n = \dim M$ and if $p > n$ then all four terms are $0$. Letting $y$ be
a indeterminate, the above equation holds with each term multiplied by $y^p$. Summing over $p\ge 0$ gives:
\[
  \chi_y(M,E) = \chi_y(M, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1}) + \chi_y(D, E) + y\chi_y(D, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1})
\]
Now, by repeating the application of equation in~\cref{df:four-termFormula}, we can write $\chi^p(D,E)$ as a linear
combination of integers of the form $\chi^q(M, A)$ where $A$ is will be an appropriate complex vector bundle over $M$. For example:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:110}
  \chi^0(D, E) = \chi^0(M,E) - \chi^0(M, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1})
\end{equation}
To calculate the last term, we use Serre duality and use:
\[
  \chi_1(M,E) = \chi_1(M, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1}) + \chi_1(D, E) + \chi_1(D, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1})
\]
and replace $E$ with $E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1}$ in equation~(\ref{eq:110}) to get:
\[
  \chi^1(D,E) = \chi^1(M, E) - \chi^1(M, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1}) - \chi^0(M, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-1}) + \chi^0(M,
  E\otimes \CL(D)^{-2})
\]
Recursively continuing this, we get:
\[
  \chi^p(D, E) = \sum_{i=0}^p (-1)^i [\chi^{p-i}(M, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-i}) - \chi^{p-i}(M, E\otimes
  \CL(D)^{-(i+1)}]
\]
This gives an important equation. These relation's do not necessarily hold for a general complex compact
manifold, in particular it is not guaranteed the right hand side cancels out, while the left hand side
will be zero for $p> n$. If $M$ is K\"ahler, or can be embedded into projective space, the relations hold, a key fact
relying on GAGA results that shall be in the next section.

Combining these together in a generating function, we get:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:11}
  \chi_y(D, E) = \sum_{i=0}^\infty (-y)^i [\chi_y(M, E\otimes \CL(D)^{-i}) - \chi_y (M, E\otimes
  \CL(D)^{-(i+1)}]
\end{equation}

\section{$\chi_y$-characteristic and Splitting Principle}\label{sec:virtualEulerChar}

In equation~(\ref{eq:11}), the right hand side is a complicated equation. The goal of this section is to show
that the formula can be represented in terms of manifolds given by line bundles so that we may apply the splitting
principle.

The idea behind the following section goes as follows. Let's say $V \hookrightarrow M$ is complex codimension
$1$. Then what is the relation between $\chi(M, E)$ and $\chi(V, E$)? What if we have a sequence $V_r
\hookrightarrow V_{r-1} \hookrightarrow \cdots \hookrightarrow V_1 \hookrightarrow M$ codimension $1$ at
each inclusion. Can we relate $\chi(M, E)$ to $V_r$ in terms of the information given by $V_1, ..., V_r$? Ideally, we can define some:
\[
  \chi((V_1, ..., V_r), E) = \cdots
\]
which will give us the Euler characteristic of $V_r$ given the information of the intersection. Converesely,
it would be good if there exists functions $F_i$ such that:
\[
  \chi(M, E) = \sum_i F_i(V_1,.., V_r, E)
\]
which allows us to link the property of $\chi(M,E)$ to simpler sub-manifolds. We shall do exactly this in this
section.

The greatest advantage of this is that as each of these manifolds are codimension one with respect to the next
one, so we can associate to them line bundles, and the line bundles are in bijective correspondence with
cohomology classes (as $M$ is projective). Thus, given a set of line bundles $\{L_1, ..., L_r\}$, we get a
filtering $V_1 \sse V_2 \sse \cdots \sse V_r \sse M$ allowing to reduce the eventual proof of Hirzebruch
Riemann Roch to a proof on line bundles; we can also think of each $V_r$ as the intersection of $M$ with some
hyper-plane (see \textcolor{red}{ref:HERE}). As it will turn out, the result is independent of order,
and hence it is well-defined to take the set $\{L_1, ..., L_r\}$ instead of the tuple $(L_1, ..., L_r)$.

By using the splitting principle, given $TM$ of $M$ splits into $A_1, ..., A_n$, We aim to find an equation
for $\chi(M, E)$ in terms of the line bundles.

A quick word must be given in terms of the reformulation of this in the Grothendieck Riemann Roch. The
inclusion $j: V \to M$ will be used to pullback a cohomology class $j^*: H^*(M) \to H^*(V)$ giving
a contra-variant theory. However, using Fulton's intersection theory we instead would take $j_*: A^*(V) \to
A^*(M)$; this is the \emph{Gysin map}. As the Grothendieck Riemann Roch is a purely algebraic proof, Gysin map
will be the ``correct'' generalization to scheme theory. Note that the Grothendieck Riemann Roch will note
require to keep track of the set of line bundles $\{A_1, ..., A_n\}$ as the proof technique hinges on
a very different approach (see section~\ref{sec:GRR}).

\subsection{Arithmetic's}

Let us represent the arithmetic of the intersection. Let $M$ be a smooth manifold, $\{L_1, ..., L_r\}$ be
associated line bundles. Define:
\[
  R = \Z[e, \ell_1, \ell_2, ..., \ell_r, \ell_1^{-1}, \ell_1^{-2}, ...,\ell_r^{-1}]
\]
where each $\ell_i$ if a free variables that should be thought of as  corresponding to a line bundle $L_i$ of
$M$. Define a homomorphism $R \to \Z[[y]]$ by defining on the generators:
\begin{align*}
  h^M(e^{m}\cdot \ell_1^{m_1}\cdots \ell_r^{m_r}) &= \chi(M, E^{m} \otimes L_1^{m_1}\otimes \cdots \otimes L_r^{m_r})\\
  h_y^M(e^{m}\cdot \ell_1^{m_1}\cdots \ell_r^{m_r}) &= \chi_y(M, E^{m} \otimes L_1^{m_1}\otimes \cdots \otimes L_r^{m_r})
\end{align*}
where the powers should be taken as taken the tensor product. Then by some elementary algebra this extends to
a $\Z[[y]]$-module $R[[y]]$ with homomorphism extending to $R[[y]]$; label it $h$ as well. By definition:
\[
  h^M(1) = \chi(M) \qquad \text{and} \qquad h_y^M(1) = \chi_y(M)
\]
By using the results of the previous section, given a divisor $D$, we an define a $\Z[[y]]$-homomorphism
\[
  h_y^D(e^{m}\cdot \ell_1^{m_1}\cdots \ell_r^{m_r}) = \chi_y(D, E^{m} \otimes L_1^{m_1}\otimes \cdots \otimes
  L_r^{m_r})
\]
where like before the line bundles have a natural restriction to $\CL(D)$. By equation~(\ref{eq:11}):
\[
  \chi_y(D, E) = h_y^D \left(e\frac{1-d^{-1}}{1+yd^{-1}}\right)
\]
and by~\cref{th:splitPrinEulerCharAndDivisor}:
\[
  \chi(D) = h^D(1-d^{-1}) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \chi(D, E) = h^D(e(1- d^{-1}))
\]
By taking successive ``divisors of divisor'', we multiply by further factors of $\frac{1-d^{-1}}{1+yd^{-1}}$,
motivating the following definition:

\index{Virtual Characteristic}\index{Euler Characteristic!Virtual}
\begin{defn}{Virtual Characteristic}{virtualChar}
  \[
  \chi_y(\{L_1, ..., L_r\}, E)_M = h_y\left(e \prod_{i=1}^r \frac{1-\ell_i^{-1}}{1 + yl_i^{-1}}\right)
\]
\end{defn}

If $E = \mathbbm{1}$, it shall simply be denoted as $\chi_y(\{L_1, ..., L_r\})_M$. By the construction of the
virtual character, it is clear that it respects the same property as the usual $\chi_y$ character:
\[
  \chi_y(\{L_1, ..., L_r\}, E)_M = \sum_{p=0}^\infty \chi^p(\{L_1, ..., L_r\}, E)_My^p
\]
By taking $\chi_M = \chi^0_M$ we can write:
\[
  \chi(\{L_1, ..., L_r\}, E)_M = h \left(e \prod_{i=1}^r (1-\ell_i^{-1})\right)
\]
An important concrete example would then be:
\[
  \chi(L)_M = \chi(M) - \chi(M, L^{-1})
\]
Notice that we are subtracting; this motivates the term ``virtual'' as we are treating $L^{-1}$ as an
``inverse'' vector bundles, a notion well-defined in $K$-theory.

Let us now find $\chi_y$ in terms of virtual characteristics. By definition of $h$, we immediately get:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:4'}
  \chi_y(D, E) = \chi_y(\{\CL(D)\}, E)_M
\end{equation}
As $\chi_y(D,E)$ is a polynomial in $n-1$ terms, so is the right hand side. We shall extend this for larger
when $N = D_1\cap\cdots \cap D_r$ which shall give a  $n-r$ degree polynomial when $M$ is a nonsingular
variety (\cref{th:inductionCase}).

\begin{thm}{Virtual Characteristic Divisor Restriction}{virtCharDivRest}
  Let $M, E, L_1, ..., L_r$ as usual, and $D$ a  divisor of $M$, $\CL(D) = L_1$. Then:
  \[
    \chi_y(\{L_1, ..., L_r\}, E)_M = \chi_y( \{(L_1)_D, ..., (L_r)_D\}, E_D)_D
  \]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  Let:
\[
    R(x) = \frac{1-x^{-1}}{1+yx^{-1}}
\]
Then by definition
\[
    \chi_M((L_2)_D,\ldots,(L_r)_D|,E_D)_D = h^M\left(e\prod_{i=2}^r R(\ell_i)\right)
\]
It follows from our work above that:
\[
    h^M(e^m \ell_1^{h_1}\ldots \ell_r^{h_r}) = \hat{h}_M(e^m\mu \ell_1^{h_1}\ldots \ell_r^{h_r}R(\ell_1))
\]
Then by letting $t=R(\ell_1)$ we get:
\[
    h^M\left(e\prod_{i=2}^r R(\ell_i)\right) = h^M\left(e\prod_{i=1}^r R(\ell_i)\right)
    = \chi_M(L_1,\ldots,L_r|,E)_M
\]
\end{Proof}


\begin{cor}{Virtual Characteristic and Trivial Bundle}{virtualCharandTrivialBundle}
  Let $M, E, L_1, ..., L_r$ as usual, and assume one $L_i = \mathbbm{1}$ is the trivial bundle. THen:
  \[
    \chi_y(\{L_1, ..., L_r\}, E)_M = 0
  \]
\end{cor}

\begin{Proof}
  immediate consequence
\end{Proof}

\begin{thm}{Algebra of Product of Line Bundles}{algebraofProdofLineBundles}
  Let $M$ be a compact complex manifold, $E$ a complex vector bundle, $L_1, ..., L_r, A,B$ complex line
  bundles. Then:
  \begin{align*}
\chi_y(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,A\otimes B\},E)_M &=\\
               &\chi_y(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,A\},E)_M\\
               &+ \chi_y(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,B\},E)_M\\
               &+ (y-1)\chi_y(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,A,B\},E)_M\\
               &- y\chi_y(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,A,B,A\otimes B\},E)_M
\end{align*}
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  Let $M$ be a compact complex manifold, $E$ a complex analytic vector bundle over $M$, and $L_i$, $A$, $B$ complex analytic line bundles over $M$. Then the following functional equation holds for the virtual $\chi_y$-characteristic:

  \begin{align*}
  \chi_y&(\{L, A \otimes B\}, E)_M = \\
  &\chi_y(\{L, A\}, E)_M + \chi_y(\{L, B \}, E)_M + (y - 1)\chi_y(\{L, A,
  B\}, E)_M - y\chi_y(\{L, A, B, A \otimes B\}, E)_M
  \end{align*}
  By definition:
  \[
  \chi_y(L_1, \dots, L_r |_M, E) = h_y^M(e \cdot \prod_{i=1}^r R(\ell_i))
  \]
  where the function $R(x)$ is given by:
  \[
  R(x) = \frac{x-1}{x+y}
  \]
  Starting with the case of a single lien bundle $L$. Let the term $u$ correspond to the common parts of the
  bundles in the equation:
  \[
  u = e \cdot R(\ell)
  \]
  Now, we can express each term in the theorem's main equation using this notation:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $\chi_y(F, A \otimes B |_M, E) = h_y^M(u \cdot R(ab))$
      \item $\chi_y(F, A |_M, E) = h_y^M(u \cdot R(a))$
      \item $\chi_y(F, B |_M, E) = h_y^M(u \cdot R(b))$
      \item $\chi_y(F, A, B |_M, E) = h_y^M(u \cdot R(a)R(b))$
      \item $\chi_y(F, A, B, A \otimes B |_M, E) = h_y^M(u \cdot R(a)R(b)R(ab))$
  \end{enumerate}
  The theorem states:
  \[
  h_y^M(u \cdot R(ab)) = h_y^M(u \cdot R(a)) + h_y^M(u \cdot R(b)) + (y-1)h_y^M(u \cdot R(a)R(b)) - y \cdot h_y^M(u \cdot R(a)R(b)R(ab))
  \]
  Since $h_y^M$ is a homomorphism, it is linear with respect to polynomials in $y$. We can thus factor out $h_y^M(u \cdot \dots)$ from the right-hand side:
  \[
  h_y^M(u \cdot R(ab)) = h_y^M\left(u \cdot \left[R(a) + R(b) + (y-1)R(a)R(b) - yR(a)R(b)R(ab)\right]\right)
  \]
  Therefore, the theorem is proven if the following algebraic identity holds:
  \[
  R(ab) = R(a) + R(b) + (y-1)R(a)R(b) - yR(a)R(b)R(ab)
  \]
  and indeed:
  \begin{align*}
    &\ R(a) + R(b) + (y-1)R(a)R(b) - yR(a)R(b)R(ab)\\
  &= \frac{a-1}{a+y} + \frac{b-1}{b+y} + (y-1)\frac{(a-1)(b-1)}{(a+y)(b+y)} - y \frac{(a-1)(b-1)}{(a+y)(b+y)} \frac{ab-1}{ab+y} \\
  &= \frac{(a-1)(b+y) + (b-1)(a+y) + (y-1)(a-1)(b-1)}{(a+y)(b+y)} - y \frac{(a-1)(b-1)(ab-1)}{(a+y)(b+y)(ab+y)} \\
  &= \frac{ab+ay-b-y + ab+by-a-y + (y-1)(ab-a-b+1)}{(a+y)(b+y)} - \dots \\
  &= \frac{2ab+a(y-1)+b(y-1)-2y + (y-1)(ab-a-b+1)}{(a+y)(b+y)} - \dots \\
  &= \frac{2ab+ay-a+by-b-2y + yab-ya-yb+y-ab+a+b-1}{(a+y)(b+y)} - \dots \\
  &= \frac{ab(1+y) - (y+1)}{(a+y)(b+y)} - \dots \\
  &= \frac{(y+1)(ab-1)}{(a+y)(b+y)} - y \frac{(a-1)(b-1)(ab-1)}{(a+y)(b+y)(ab+y)} \\
  &= \frac{ab-1}{(a+y)(b+y)} \left[ (y+1) - y \frac{(a-1)(b-1)}{ab+y} \right] \\
  &= \frac{ab-1}{(a+y)(b+y)} \left[ \frac{(y+1)(ab+y) - y(ab-a-b+1)}{ab+y} \right] \\
  &= \frac{ab-1}{(a+y)(b+y)(ab+y)} \left[ yab+y^2+ab+y - (yab-ya-yb+y) \right] \\
  &= \frac{ab-1}{(a+y)(b+y)(ab+y)} \left[ y^2+ab+ya+yb \right] \\
  &= \frac{ab-1}{(a+y)(b+y)(ab+y)} \left[ (a+y)(b+y) \right] \\
  &= \frac{ab-1}{ab+y} \\
  &= R(ab)
  \end{align*}
  Then, by the product formula in the definition of the virtual characteristic, notice we get the equation (letting $\CL = L_1, ..., L_r$):
\[
  \chi(\{\CL, A\otimes B\}, E)_M = \chi(\{\CL,A\}, E)_M + \chi(\{\CL, B\}, E)_M - \chi(\{\CL, A,B\}, E)_M
\]
  as we sought to show.
\end{Proof}

Let us now say that every vector bundle splits on $M$. Then $TM$ splits into line bundles $A_1\oplus \cdots
\oplus A_m$. Then $\bigwedge^p TM$ splits into $\binom{m}{p}$ line bundles of the form:
\[
  A_{i_1}^{-1}\otimes \cdots \otimes A_{i_p}^{-1} \qquad (i_1 < \cdots < i_p)
\]
By~\cref{th:splittingPrincipleandEulerChar}:
\begin{align*}
  \chi^p(M, E) &= \chi(M, E\otimes \bigwedge^p TM)\\
               &= \sum_{i_1 < \cdots < i_p} \chi(M, E\otimes A_{i_1}^{-1}\otimes \cdots \otimes A_{i_p}^{-1})
\end{align*}

  By the definition of
\[
  \chi_y(M, E) = \sum h_y^M \left(e \prod_{i=1}^m (1+ya_i^{-1})\right)
\]

This shall now give us a decomposition of $\chi$ in terms of the virtual character

\begin{thm}{Virtual Character Decomposition}{virtualCharacterDecomp}
  Let $M$ be a complex manifolds where $TM$ splits completely into lienar terms intos $A_1, ..., A_m$. Let $E$
  be a complex vector bundle. Then:
  \[
    (1+y)^m \chi(M, E) = \sum_{l=0}^m y^l \sum_{i_1 < \cdots < i_l} \chi_y(\{A_{i_1}, ..., A_{i_l}\}, E)_M
  \]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
    Writing in term $h_y^M$:
  \begin{align*}
      \sum_{l=0}^m y^l \sum_{i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_l} h_y^M(eR(a_{i_1})\ldots R(a_{i_l})) &= h_Y^m\left(\sum_{l=0}^m y^l \sum_{i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_l} eR(a_{i_1})\ldots R(a_{i_l})\right) \\
      &= h_y^M\left(e\prod_{i=1}^m(1+yR(a_i))\right) \\
      &= h_y^M\left(e\prod_{i=1}^m(1+y)(1+ya_i^{-1})^{-1}\right) \\
      &= (1+y)^mh_y^M\left(e\prod_{i=1}^m(1+ya_i^{-1})^{-1}\right)
  \end{align*}
  Thus, we get
  \[
      h_y^M(e^m a_1^{h_1}a_2^{h_2}\ldots a_r^{h_r}) = h^M\left(e^m a_1^{h_1}a_2^{h_2}\ldots a_r^{h_r}\prod_{i=1}^m(1+ya_i^{-1})\right)
  \]
  Then, letting:
  \[
      t = \prod_{i=1}^m(1+ya_i^{-1})
  \]
  gives:
  \begin{align*}
      (1+y)^mh_Y^m\left(w\prod_{i=1}^m(1+ya_i^{-1})^{-1}\right) &= (1+y)^mh\left(w\prod_{i=1}^m(1+ya_i^{-1})^{-1}(1+ya_i^{-1})\right) \\
      &= (1+y)^mh(w) = (1+y)^m\chi(V,W)
  \end{align*}
  as we sought to show
\end{Proof}






\subsection{Relating Euler Characteristic to Chern Numbers}\label{sec:virtualEulerCharOnProjVar}

Finally, let us show that the virtual characteristic with $r$ linear bundles is an $n-r$ polynomial, and that
evaluating when $n=r$ works the same as evaluating $M$ at the cup-product of the respective $\ell_i \in h^2(m
,\Z)$.

\begin{lem}{0-dimensional Case}{0-dimensionalCase}
  Le $M = \{p_1, ..., p_k\}$ be a $0$-dimensional manifold, $E$ an $n$-dimensional vector bundle over $M$, $L_1, ..., L_r$ line
  bundles over $M$. Then:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $\chi_y(M, E) = nk$
  \item $\chi_y(\{L_1, ... L_r\}, E) = 0$ for $r \ge 1$
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lem}

\begin{Proof}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $\chi_y(M, E) = \chi(M,E) = \dim H^0(M, E) = nk$
    \item By Bass-Serre theorem (see~\cite{NateKTheory}\footnote{We actually prove a special case of
      Bass-Serre in~\cref{th:atiyah-serreOnVecBundles} which is a ``geometric'' proof as compared to the
    algebraic proof in the cited result}), every line bundle is trivial, so by~\cref{co:virtualCharandTrivialBundle}
    \[
      \chi_y(\{L_1, ..., L_r\}, E) = 0
    \]
  \end{enumerate}
\end{Proof}


In general, recall $\chi_y(M,E)$ is a polynomial as the power series must terminate by definition. Let us prove by
induction on the dimension $n$ on $M$ that the virtual $\chi_y$-characteristic is also a polynomial when $M$
is a complete variety.


\begin{thm}{Induction Case}{inductionCase}
  Let $M$ be a complete variety of complex dimension $n$. Let $E$ be a $q$-dimensional complex vector
  bundle, and let $L_1, ..., L_r$ be complex line bundles over $M$. Then:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item For $r > 0$, $\chi_y(\{L_1, ..., L_r\}, E)_M = 0$ for $r > n$.
    \item For $r \le n$, $\chi_y(\{L_1, ..., L_r\}, E)_M$ is a polynomial of degree $\le n-r$ in $y$ with
      integer coefficients
     \item If $r = n \ge 1$, then
       \[
       \chi_y(\{L_1, ..., L_n\}, E)_M = \chi(L_1, ..., L_n\})_M = \int_M q\smile l_1\smile l_2\smile \cdots \smile
       l_n
       \]
       where $l_i \in H^2(M, \Z)$ are the cohomology classes of $L_i$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}
We shall often use $\cdot$ instead of $\smile$ notation to de-clutter notation.

\begin{Proof}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Lemma~\cref{lm:0-dimensionalCase} prove it for $\dim M=0$. Suppose it is true for $\dim M<n$.
    By~\cref{th:kodairaVanishingThmII} there are divisors $D$ and $S$ of $M$ such that
    $\CL(D)=L_1\otimes\CL(S)$. Then by~\cref{th:virtualCharacterDecomp}:
    \begin{align*}
      \chi_M&(\{ \CL(D),L_2,\cdots,L_r \},E)\\
    &= \chi_M(\{ L_1,\cdots,L_r \},E) + \chi_M(\{ \CL(S),L_2,\cdots,L_r \},E) \\
                          &\ + (y-1)\chi_M(\{\CL(S),L_1,\cdots,L_r\},E)
                          - y\chi_M(\{\CL(D),\CL(S),L_1,\cdots,L_r\},E)
  \end{align*}
We must show the 2nd term has degree $\leq n-r$. Note that
induction hypothesis, together with~\cref{th:kodairaVanishingThmII} shows 1st, 3rd, 4th and 5th term all have
degree $\leq n-r$ and vanish for $r>n$. Note that if $r=1$ then 1st term is $\chi_M(D,E_D)$ and 3rd term is
$\chi_M(S,E_S)$; these terms are polynomials of degree $\leq n-1$ by the definition of the (non-virtual)
$\chi_M$-characteristic. Therefore 2nd term is a polynomial of degree $\leq n-r$ and zero for $r>n$.
\item By~\cref{th:kodairaVanishingThmII} there are  divisors $D$ and $S$ of $M$
  such that $\CL(D)=L_1\otimes\CL(D)$.

Then for $n\ge 2$, (1) gives:
\[
    \chi(\{ \CL(D),L_2,\ldots,\ell_n \},E) = \chi(\{ L_1,L_2,\ldots,\ell_n \},E) + \chi(\{
    \CL(D),L_2,\ldots,\ell_n \},E)
\]
and for $n=1$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:indHypToThis}
    \chi(\{ \CL(D) \},E) = \chi(\{ L_1 \},E) + \chi(\{ \CL(D) \},E)
\end{equation}

Then by lemma~\cref{lm:0-dimensionalCase} the $n=1$ case gives
\[
    \chi(\{ L_1 \},E) = qd - qs = q\ell_1[M_1]
\]
where $d$, $s$ are the number of points of $D$, $S$. Therefore (2) is true for $\dim M=1$. Now for the sake of
induction supposed it is is proved for $1\leq\dim M<n$. We now apply the induction hypothesis to
equation~(\ref{eq:indHypToThis}). First, recall that if $h \in H^2(M, \Z)$ is the cohomology class
representing the divisor $D$ then $c_1([D]) = h$. If $j: D \hookrightarrow M$ is an inclusion (treating $D$ as
a communions $1$ submanifold), then for any $x \in H^{n-2}(M, \Z)$:
\[
  \int_D j^*(x) = \int_M h\smile x = \int_M hx
\]
Then by~\cref{th:virtCharDivRest}, we get:
\begin{align*}
    \chi(\{ L_1,L_2,\ldots,\ell_n \},E) &= \int_D q\cdot(\ell_2\ldots \ell_n)_D - \int_S q\cdot(\ell_2\ldots \ell_n)_S \\
    &= \int_M q\cdot c_1(\CL(D))\ell_2\ldots \ell_n - \int_Mq\cdot c_1(\CL(S))\ell_2\ldots \ell_n \\
    &= \int_M q\cdot \ell_1\ell_2\ldots \ell_n
\end{align*}
as we sought to show.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Proof}

When $n = 1$, this is in fact Riemann-Roch for curves! Let $M = C$ be a curve, $L$ a line bundle with
cohomology class $l  \in H^2(C, \Z)$. Then the $\chi$-genus of $L^{-1}$ is:
\[
  \chi(L^{-1}) = \int_C-l
\]
Then: $\chi(L) = \chi(C) - \chi(C, L^{-1})$ and so we get:
\[
  \chi(C, L) = \chi(L) + \int_C l
\]
Next by using~\cref{th:symmetryofHodgeNumberOnKahlerManifold} we get:
\[
  \chi(C) = 1 - g
\]
By classical algebraic topology, $\int_C l$ is called the \emph{degree} of $L$, which (naturally) is equal to
$\deg(L)$. Using Poincar\'e duality we get:
\[
  \chi(C, L) = \dim H^0(C, L) - \dim H^1(C, L0 = \dim H^0(C, L) - \dim H^0(C, K\otimes L^{-1})
\]
Combining it all together, we obtain:
\[
  \dim H^0(C, L) - \dim H^0(C, K\otimes L^{-1}) = 1 - g + \deg(L)
\]
i.e., classical Riemann Roch!


Finally, we must show the virtual Euler characteristic characterizes these relations:

\begin{thm}{Uniqueness Result}{uniquenessResult}
  let $M,E, L_1, ..., L_r$ be a usual, $\CL = \{L_1, ..., L_r\}$. Let $G$ function associating each $(\CL,
  E)_M$ to a $\Q$-power series in $y$ and is independent of the order of $L_i$ (i.e. these terms must all
  commute). Then if:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $G(M, E) = \chi_y(M,E)$
    \item
  \begin{align*}
  G(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,A\otimes B\},E)_M &=\\
                 &G(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,A\},E)_M\\
                 &+ G(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,B\},E)_M\\
                 &+ (y-1)G(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,A,B\},E)_M\\
                 &- yG(\{L_1,\cdots,L_r,A,B,A\otimes B\},E)_M
  \end{align*}
  % (\textcolor{red}{I put the $y$ in red because it is not in Hirzebruch, maybe a typo maybe intentional})
  \item If $D$ is a (nonsingular) divisor of $M$ and $L_1 = \CL(D)$, then:
    \[
      G(\CL, E)_M = G(\{(L_1)_D, ..., (L_r)_D\}, E_D)_D
    \]
  \end{enumerate}
  Then for all $(\CL, E)_M$ with $r \ge 1$:
  \[
    \chi_y(\CL, E)_M = G(\CL, E)_M
  \]
  The same exact result holds when taking virtual characteristic.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  We outline the result for the Euler-characteristic, leaving the virtual characteristic as an exercise.
  First, $\chi_M$ has properties (2) and (3) and therefore the function $\chi_M-G$ has properties (2) and (3).
  It is therefore sufficient to show that any function $G'$ which satisfies (2), (3) and the additional property
  that:
  \[
    G'(M,E)=0
  \]
  must be identically zero.

  We proceed by induction. By~\cref{th:kodairaVanishingThmII} there are divisors $D$ and $T$ of $M$ such that $\CL(D)=F_1\otimes\CL(T)$.
  Then (2) implies equation
  \begin{align*}
    \chi_M&(\{ \CL(D),L_2,\cdots,L_r \},E)\\
    &= \chi_M(\{ L_1,\cdots,L_r \},E) + \chi_M(\{ \CL(S),L_2,\cdots,L_r \},E) \\
                        &\ + (y-1)\chi_M(\{\CL(S),L_1,\cdots,L_r\},E)
                        - y\chi_M(\{\CL(D),\CL(S),L_1,\cdots,L_r\},E)
  \end{align*}
  from~\cref{th:inductionCase} with $\chi_M$ replaced by $G'$. This equation has five terms, and we have to
  prove that the second term is zero. The induction hypothesis and (3) imply that terms 1, 3, 4, 5 are zero.
  For $r=1$, it is necessary to use $G'=0$ to prove that terms 1, 3 are zero. Therefore term 2 is zero.
\end{Proof}

and Todd class satisfies the same properties, and hence will have to be equal (see \textcolor{red}{ref:HERE}).

\chapter{Todd Class}\label{cha:toddGenus}

In section~\ref{sec:chernCharacter}, we defined the Chern character which gives:
\[
  \ch(E_1\oplus E_2) = \ch(E_1) + \ch(E_2) \in H^*(X, \Q)
\]
In section~\ref{sec:eulerCharAndGeneralization}, we saw that we can define the Euler characteristic for vector
bundle:
\[
  \chi(X, E_1\oplus E_2) = \chi(X, E_1) + \chi(X, E_2)
\]
This algebraic property gives us the hope that we can directly generalize Chern's Theorem to:
\[
  \chi(X, E) = \int_X \ch(E)
\]
However, this does not give the expected result:

\begin{example}{Incorrect Generalization}{incorrectGeneralization}
  Let $E = \mathbbm{1}$ be the trivial bundle. Then $\ch(\mathbbm{1}) = 1$. Now consider $X= S^2$. Then:
  \[
    2 = \chi({S^2}) = \chi({S^2}, \mathbbm{1}) \ne \int_{S^2} \ch(S^2) = 0
  \]
  showing we do not recover the Euler characteristic.
\end{example}

The problem is that we are not taking into considering the geometric information of $X$. What would be desired
is a ``correction class'' $C(TX)$ ($C$ for correction) where $TX$ is the tangent bundle where:
\[
  \chi(X, E) = \int_X \ch(E) \smile C(TX)
\]
This shall be the \emph{Todd class} $\td(X)$ where $\td(X) = C(TX)$. What properties should $\td(-)$ require? For one, we must have:
\[
  \chi(X, \mathbbm{1}) = \chi(X) = \int_X \td(X)
\]
For another, as $\chi(X\oplus Y) = \chi(X)\cdot \chi(Y)$, $\td(X\oplus Y) = \td(X)\cdot \td(Y)$, namely it is
\emph{multiplicative} on direct sums. Finally, we shall be able to reduce to proving the result  on the complex
projective spaces as demonstrated in the cobordism isomorphism in~\cref{th:isoofAlgebraicCobordismRing}, hence
it is necessary to have $\td(\CP^k)$ agree with $\chi(\CP^k)$. In particular, as we know the line bundles over
$\CP^k$, we can deduce $\td(-)$ by making it agree with the values of $\chi(-)$. First, we shall show that
$\td(-)$ will be correspond by a power series:
\[
  Q(x) = 1 + a_1x + a_2\frac{x^2}{2!} + a_3 \frac{x^3}{3!} +  \cdots
\]
which will return the appropriate polynomial of Chern classes given the tangent bundle (exactly how is explain
in section~\ref{sec:mSequence}). We shall find the first term of this series by insuring $\td(\CP^1) =
\chi(\CP^1)$. Recall that $T\CP^1 = \CO(2)$, the twisted line bundle, and $\chi(\CP^1) = 1$. Thus we want:
$\td(\CP^1) = Q\left(c_1(\CO(2))\right)$. It is easy to compute that
\[
  c_1(\CO(2)) = h \in H^2(\CP^1; \Z) \cong \Z[h]/(h^2)
\]
as $h^2 = 0$, we get:
\begin{align*}
  \chi(\CP^1) = 1 &= \int_{\CP^1} \td(\CP^1))\\
  &= \int_{\CP^1} Q(c_1(T\CP^1))\\
  &= \int_{\CP^1} Q(2h)\\
  &= \int_{\CP^1} (1 + 2a_1h) = 2a_1\\
\end{align*}
Thus, it must be that $a_1 = \frac{1}{2}$, giving us:
\[
  Q(x) = 1 + \frac{1}{2}x + a_1 \frac{x^2}{2!} + \cdots
\]
Notice that if we consider $\CO(\CP^1, \CO(d))$, then we get:
\[
  \chi(\CP^1, \CO(d)) = d+1
\]
and to compute
\[
  \ch(\CO(d)) = e^{kh} = 1 + dh
\]
as $h^2 = 0$. Thus:
\begin{align*}
  \chi(\CP^1, \CO(d) = d+1 &= \int_{\CP^1} \ch(\CO(d)) \smile \td(\CP^1))\\
  &= \int_{\CP^1} \ch(\CO(d)) \smile Q(c_1(T\CP^1))\\
  &= \int_{\CP^1} \ch(\CO(d)) \smile Q(2h)\\
  &= \int_{\CP^1} (1+2h) \smile (1 + h)\\
  &= \int_{\CP^1} [1 + (1 + d)h] = d+1
\end{align*}

Giving equality in this special case. Repeating this process again for $\CP^k$ for $k \in \N$, we shall get
the next coefficients. In section~\ref{sec:mSequence}, we go over the general algebraic method for finding
such a power series, and in~\cref{pr:coefficientofL-genusPowerSeries} we shall show how we deduce the rest of
this Todd power series. Overall, repeating this process will define $\td(-)$ and we by construction have it agree on
at least the Euler characteristic of $\CP^n$ with the standard line bundles. We shall show it is enough in the
proof of Hirzebruch Riemann Roch by taking advantage of the results on Cobordism and the splitting principle.

After defining the Todd class, we shall combine the Todd class and Chern character to create the
\emph{T-characteristic} and again show how it well-defined and has the desired properties for any vector
bundle over a space $E \to X$.

\section{Algebraic Build-up: Multiplicative sequences}\label{sec:mSequence}

As we saw with Chern and Pontryagin classes, the direct sum of bundles translates to a cup product of
characteristic classes. This gives a natural algebraic relation that we know codify for future reference. Let
$B$ be a commutative ring, and define:
\[
  \CB = B[z, p_1, p_2, ...]
\]
Define a grading given by the following:
\begin{center}
  $p_{j_1}p_{j_2}\cdots p_{j_r}$ has weight $j_1 + j_2 + \cdots + j_r$
\end{center}
Then let $\CB_k$ be the weight $k$-groups; note $\CB_0 = B$ and $\CB_r\CB_s = \CB_{r+s}$. Let $\pi(k)$ be all
partitions of $k$. Then $\CB_k$ is a $B$-module of rank $\pi(k)$.

\index{Multiplicative Sequence}
\begin{defn}{Multiplicative Sequence}{multiplicativeSequence}
  Let $\{K_j\}$ be a sequence of polynomials in indeterminates $p_i$ where $K_0 = 1$ and $K_j \in \CB_j$. Then
  the sequence $\{K_j\}$ is called a \emph{multiplicative sequence} or \emph{m-sequence} if whenever a power series is the product
  of two power series:
  \[
    1 + p_1z + p_2z^2  +\cdots = (1 + p_1'z + p_2'z^2  +\cdots)(1 + p_1''z + p_2''z^2  +\cdots)
  \]
  with indeterminates $z, p_i', p_i''$, then we get the identify:
  \[
    \sum_{j=0}^\infty K_j(p_1, p_2, ... p_j)z^j = \left(\sum_{i=1}^\infty K_i(p_1', p_2', ...
    p_i')z^i\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^\infty K_j(p_1'', p_2'', ... p_i'')z^j\right)
  \]
  that is:
  \[
    K \left(\sum_{i=0}^\infty q_iz^i\right) = \sum_{i=0}^\infty K_i(q_1, ..., q_i)z^i
  \]
  where $q_i \in \CB$ of weight $i$. In other words a sequence $\{K_j\}$ is called multiplicative if it induces an endomorphism on
  \[
    B[p_1, p_2, ...]\left[[z]\right]
  \]
\end{defn}

Given a multiplicative sequence $\{K_j\}$, we associate to it its \emph{characteristic power series} as the
image of $K(1+z)$:
\[
  K(1+z) = \sum_j^\infty K_j(1, 0, ..., 0)z^j = \sum_j^\infty b_jz^j
\]

An important property of vector bundles is that they can be pulled back over a flag manifold so that they
split into line bundles. This strategy shall be important to us, and hence we will be considering:
\[
  1 + p_1z + \cdots + p_nz^n = \prod_i^n \left(1 + \beta_iz\right)
\]
where $\beta_i$ is a symmetric polynomial in the $p_i$'s. By some elementary ring theory and theory of
symmetric functions, we can interpret $\CB$ as the ring of all symmetric functions in $\beta_1, ..., \beta_n$.


We can characterize $m$-sequences via $1$-unit power series:

\begin{prop}{Correspondence 1-unit Power Series and $m$-sequences}{corresp1-unitPowerSeriesand$m$-sequences}
  There is a bijective correspondence between $m$-sequences and formal power series with constant term $1$.
\end{prop}

\begin{Proof}
    First, let's show any $m$-sequence $\{K_j\}$ is completely determined by its characteristic power series
    \[
      Q(z) = K(1+z)
    \]
   \begin{proof}
    By decomposing:

  \[
    1 + p_1z + \cdots + p_nz^n = \prod_i^n \left(1 + \beta_iz\right)
  \]
  We get:
  \[
  \sum_{j=0}^m K_j(p_1,\ldots,p_j) z^j + \sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty} K_j(p_1,\ldots,p_m,0,\ldots,0) z^j = \prod_{i=1}^m Q(\beta_i z). \quad (6_m)
  \]
  Therefore any polynomial $K_j$ with $j \leq m$ is determined as a symmetric polynomial in the $\beta_i$ and
  hence as a polynomial in the $p_i$. This holds for arbitrary $m$, completing the proof.
  \end{proof}

  Next, let
    \[
      Q(z) = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^\infty b_iz^i \qquad b_i \in B

    be a formal power series. Then there is an associated $m$-sequence $\{K_j\}$ where $K(1+z) = Q(z)$.
  \begin{proof}
    Consider the usual decomposition:
    \[
      1 + p_1z + \cdots + p_nz^n = \prod_i^n \left(1 + \beta_iz\right)
    \]
    and consider the product $\prod_{i=1}^m Q(\beta_i z)$ where $\beta_i$ are symmetric functions. Then the
    coefficients $z^j$ are homogeneous of weight $j$. Thus, it is in $K_j^{(m)}(p_1,\ldots,p_j)$ . It follows that $K_j^{(m)}$
    does not depend on $m$ for $m \geq j$.

    Now, define $K_j = K_j^{(m)}$ for $m \geq j$. I claim $\{K_j\}$ is
    the required $m$-sequence. Indeed, the earlier proof the
    multiplicative property is true if the indeterminates $p_i'$, $p_i''$ are replaced by 0
    for large values of $i$, and so $\{K_j\}$ is an $m$-sequence. Finally applying $m = 1$ in equation:
    \[
    \sum_{j=0}^m K_j(p_1,\ldots,p_j) z^j + \sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty} K_j(p_1,\ldots,p_m,0,\ldots,0) z^j = \prod_{i=1}^m Q(\beta_i z). \quad (6_m)
    \]
    shows that $K(1 + z) = Q(z)$, completing the proof.
  \end{proof}

  Combining these, we get the correspondence
\end{Proof}

\section{Todd Class}

With the algebraic established, we can formally define the Todd class:

\index{Todd Class}
\begin{defn}{Todd Class}{toddClass}
  Let $X$ be a complex smooth manifold, and $E$ a continuous $q$-vector bundle on $X$. Let $c_i \in H^{2i}(X, \Z)$
  be its Chern classes. Then we can define the \emph{total Todd class} of $E$ by taking the Todd polynomials
  and taking:
  \[
    \td(E) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty T_j(c_1, ..., c_j)
  \]
  where $\{T_j(c_1, ..., c_j)\}$ is given by the characteristic series:
  \[
    Q(x) = \frac{x}{1-e^{-x}} = 1 = \frac{1}{2}x + \sum_{j=1}^\infty (-1)^{j-1} \frac{B_j}{(2j)!}x^{2j}
  \]
  where $B_j$ is the Bernoulli numbers.
\end{defn}
The reader can verify that the direct product of manifolds $X\times Y$ is multiplicative on the Todd class.
This means that if
\[
  0 \to E' \to E \to E'' \to 0
\]
Then:
\[
  (1 + c_1't + \cdots + c_{q'}'t^{q'})(1 + c_1''t + \cdots + c_{q''}''t^{q''}) =  1 + c_1t + \cdots + c_{q}t^{q}
\]
and so:
\[
  \td(E)(t) = \td(E')(t)\cdot \td(E'')(t)
\]
Mirroring the property of the Euler Characteristic.

To write down the $m$-sequence, recall that the $m$-sequence of the
characteristic power series $Q(z) = \frac{2\sqrt{z}}{\sinh(2\sqrt{z}}$ is has as the first few terms:
\[
  A_1 = \frac{-2}{3},\ A_2 = \frac{2}{45} (-4p_2 + 7p_1^2)\ A_3 = \frac{-4}{3^2\cdot 5\cdot 7} \left(16p_3
  - 44p_2p_1 + 31 p_1^3\right)
\]
Then note that :
\[
  \frac{x}{1-e^{-x}} = \exp \left(\frac{1}{2}x\right) \frac{\frac{1}{2}x}{\sinh \frac{1}{2}x}
\]
By our earlier work:
\[
  \td(E\oplus E') = \td(E)\td(E')
\]
In the special case where $q= 1$ and $c_1(E) = d \in H^2(X, \Z)$, then:
\[
  \td(E) = \frac{d}{1-e^{-d}}
\]
Then for a general $q$-bundle we can apply the splitting principle. In terms of the Todd class, we get
define:
\[
  \sum_{j=0}^q c_jx^j = \prod_{i=1}^q (1 + \gamma_ix)
\]
Thus, when dealing with a general complex vector bundle $E$ and take its Chern roots $\gamma_i$:
\[
  \td(E) = \prod_{i=1}^q \frac{\gamma_i}{1-e^{-\gamma_i}}
\]
Let us compare this to the usual results we get about the Chern class: it is the natural ``dual'' of the Todd
class in the following way:
\begin{thm}{Borel-Serre Identity}{relatingToddClassandChernCharacter}
  Let $E$ be a continuous $\GL_q(\C)$-bundle over $X$. Then:
  \[
    \sum_{r=0}^q (-1)^r \ch \left(\bigwedge^rE^*\right) = (\td(E))^{-1}c_q(E)
  \]
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  Proof: If $\sum_{j=0}^q c_j(E) x^j = \prod_{i=1}^q (1 + \gamma_i x)$ by the properties of Chern classes

\[
    \text{ch}\left(\bigwedge^r E^*\right) = \sum e^{-(\gamma_{i_1} + \cdots + \gamma_{i_r})}
\]
where the sum is over all combinations $i_1, \ldots, i_r$ with $1 \leq i_1 < \cdots < i_r \leq q$. Therefore
\[
    \sum_{r=0}^q (-1)^r \text{ch}\bigwedge^r E^* = \prod_{i=1}^q (1-e^{-\gamma_i}) = (\gamma_1 \cdots \gamma_q) \prod_{i=1}^q \frac{1-e^{-\gamma_i}}{\gamma_i} = (\text{td}(E))^{-1} c_q(E).
\]
\end{Proof}

  \]

\section{(Oriented) Cobordism Ring}\label{sec:pontryaginNum}



Let $V^n$ be an oriented compact differentiable manifold where $n$ is the dimension. As $V$ is compact and
orientable, it is a cycle, and by cohomology theory $H^n(V^n, \Z)$ is generated by one element. The value of the
$n$-dimensional cohomology class $x$ on the fundamental cycle $V^n$ is denoted $x[V^n]$.  If the coefficients
of the cohomology group are in $A$, then:
\[
  x \in H^n(V^n, A) \qquad x[V^n] \in A
\]

We can extend this by tensoring by a group $B$ so that :
\[
  x[V^n] \in A\otimes B \qquad x \in H^n(V^n, A)\otimes B \cong H^n(V^n, B)
\]
For our purposes, let $n = 4k$ and let $p_i \in H^{4i}(V^{4k}, \Z)$ be the Pontryagin classes of $V^{4k}$
(i.e. of its tangent bundle). We
get a natural grading by the indices $i$ so that given $k = j_1 + j_2 + \cdots + j_r$ we get the product
$p_{j_1}\cdots p_{j_r}$ and an integer (as $A = \Z)$:
\[
  p_{j_1}\cdots p_{j_r}[V^{4k}]
\]
Let $\pi(k)$ be the number of distinct partitions of $k$.

\index{Pontryagin Numbers}\index{Chern Numbers}
\begin{defn}{Pontryagin Numbers and Chern Number}{pontryaginNumbers}
  The \emph{Pontryagin numbers} are the $\pi(k)$ numbers :
  \[
    p_{j_1}\cdots p_{j_r}[V^{4k}]
  \]
  where $n = 4k$. If $n$ is not divisible by $4$, then the Pontryagin number is $0$.

  \qn
  Similarly the \emph{Chern numbers} of a complex manifold $M$ (with an almost complex structure so that we
  are given a particular orientation) are all:
  \[
    c_{j_1}\cdots c_{j_r}[V^{2k}]
  \]
  where $n = j_1 + j_2 + \cdots + j_r$ and the $j_i$ run through all partitions.
\end{defn}

By definition of the Euler class, if $M_n$ is an oriented almost complex manifold, $c_n[M_n] = \chi(M_n,
TM_n)$, showing that the Pontryagin/Chern numbers are ``generalizations'' of the Euler characteristic. We
shall focus on Pontryagin numbers as we shall need them for the classification result, and return to Chern
numbers once we cover the Todd genus in chapter~\ref{cha:toddGenus}.

We can define an equivalence relation where two manifolds $M\sim_\cp N$ if they have the same dimension and
are the same Pontryagin number. Note that if the dimension is not divisible by $4$, all manifolds are in the
same equivalence relation given by $\sim_\cp$ as they are all $0$.

If $M, N$ are disjoint, the Pontryagin numbers of $M\dcup N$ is the sum of the Pontryagin numbers of $M,N$;
this comes straight from the properties of characteristic classes. Let us show the same works for the product
of manifolds. Now let $M,N$ be smooth $n =4k$ dimensional manifolds. Then by classical differential geometry we have
$T(M\times N) = \pi_M^*(TM)\times \pi_N^*(TM)$. Then:
\[
  (\pi_M^*(x)\pi_N^*(y))[M\times N] = x[M]\cdot y[N] \qquad \forall x \in H^n(M,\Z)\otimes B,\ y \in H^m(N,\Z)\otimes B
\]
Thus, we can extend this results to characteristic sequences of Pontryagin classes.
Considering the Pontryagin classes of $M,N$ and $M\times N$:
\begin{align*}
  &1, \cp_1, \cp_2, ...\\
  &1, \cp_1', \cp_2', ...\\
  &1, \cp_1'', \cp_2'', ...\\
\end{align*}
By the properties of characteristic classes we get:
\[
  1 + p_1'' + p_2'' + \cdots = (1 + p_1 + p_2 + \cdots)(1 + p_1' + p_2' + \cdots)
\]
Or, as a generating function:
\[
  \sum_{k=0}^\infty p_k'' z^k = \sum_{i=0}^\infty \pi_M^*(p_i) z^i \sum_{j=0}^\infty \pi_N^*(p_j') z^j \mod \text{torsion}.
\]
Thus, define the following:
\index{Algebraic K-genus}
\begin{defn}{Algebraic K-genus}{algebraicK-genus}
  Let $\{K_j\}$ be an $m$-sequence and let $M$ be a $4\ell$-manifold. Then the \emph{algebraic K-genus} of
  $M$ is evaluated is:
  \[
    K(M) = K_\ell(\cp_1, ..., \cp_\ell)[M] = \int_M K_\ell(\cp_1, ..., \cp_\ell)
  \]
  where $\cp_1, ..., \cp_\ell$ are the Pontryagin classes of $TM$.
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}{Properties of Algebraic Genus}{propofAlgebraicGenus}
  Let $\{K_\ell\}$ be an $m$-sequence, $M,N$ be oriented $4\ell$-manifolds. Then:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $K(M\dcup N) = K(M)+K(N)$
    \item $K(M\times N) = K(M)\cdot K(N)$
    \item $K(-M) = -K(M)$ where $-M$ is the reverse orientation put on $M$.
  \end{enumerate}
  Thus, $K(-)$ respects the equivalence relation $\sim_\cp$.
\end{prop}

\begin{Proof}
  (1) and (2) follow from the above discussion, (3) follows elementary cohomology.
\end{Proof}

\section{Intersection Form: Index and Hirzebruch Signature Theorem}

On any $4k$-dimensional manifold, there is a natural generalization of the symplectic form known as the
\emph{intersection form}: given a choice of $x,y \in H^{2k}(M, \R)$, Define:
\[
  Q(x,y) = x\smile y[M^n]
\]
As $x\smile y \in H^4k$:
\[
  (x\smile y) = (-1)^{k^2}(y\smile x) = (y\smile x)
\]
show that the form is \emph{symmetric} (rather than anti-symmetric), which is why we are working with
$4$-dimensional manifolds. Thus, the eigenvalues of the corresponding matrix representations are always
\emph{real} and there always exists an orthogonal basis. If $e_p$ and $e_n$ represents the number of positive
and negative eigenvalues, then:
\index{Index}
\begin{defn}{Index}{index}
  \[
  \tau(Q) = e_p - e_n
\]
\end{defn}

The most important result about the index is that it is a \emph{genus}:

\begin{thm}{Index is Genus}{indexisGenus}
  Let $\tau$ be the index function. Then:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $\tau(M\dcup N) = \tau(M) + \tau(N)$
    \item $\tau(M)\times N) =\tau(M)\cdot \tau(N)$
    \item $\tau(-M) = -\tau(M)$
    \item If $M$ is nullcobordant, $\tau(M) = 0$
  \end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
Proof. I) follows immediately from the definitions of $V^n + W^n$ and $-V^n$.

II) is known ({\color{red} THOM [2]}) but is given there without proof. We therefore prove II) in full. Let $M^{4k} = V^n \times W^m$. Then
\[
    H^{2k}(M^{4k}, \mathbf{R}) \cong \sum_{s=0}^{2k} H^s(V^n, \mathbf{R}) \otimes H^{2k-s}(W^m, \mathbf{R}).
\]

Elements $x, y \in H^{2k}(M^{4k}, \mathbf{R})$ are said to be orthogonal if $x y[M^{4k}] = 0$. Introduce bases $\{v_i\}$ for $H^s(V^n, \mathbf{R})$ and $\{w_j\}$ for $H^t(W^m, \mathbf{R})$ such that $v_i^s v_j^{n-s}[V^n] = \delta_{ij}$ for $s \neq \frac{n}{2}$ and $w_i^t w_j^{m-t}[W^m] = \delta_{ij}$ for $t \neq \frac{m}{2}$.

Now consider the group $A = H^{\frac{n}{2}}(V^n, \mathbf{R}) \otimes H^{\frac{m}{2}}(W^m, \mathbf{R})$, taking $A = 0$ if $n$ and $m$ are odd. Then $A$ is orthogonal to the subgroup $B$ of $H^{2k}(M^{4k}, \mathbf{R})$, which consists of all elements of the summation (1) in which no elements of $A$ occur. As a basis for the group $B$ we can take $\{v_i^s \otimes w_j^{2k-s}\}, (0 \leq s \leq n, s \neq \frac{n}{2})$. Now
\begin{align*}
(v_i^s \otimes w_j^{2k-s})(v_{i'}^{s'} \otimes w_{j'}^{2k-s'})[M^{4k}] &= \pm 1 \text{ if } s + s' = n, i = i', j = j' \\
&= 0 \text{ otherwise.}
\end{align*}

It follows that, with respect to this basis, the restriction of the bilinear form $x y[M^{4k}]$ to $B$ is represented by a matrix with blocks $\pm \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ down the diagonal and zero elsewhere. Therefore the index of the restriction of $x y[M^{4k}]$ to $B$ is 0. Since $A$ and $B$ are orthogonal, $\tau(M^{4k})$ is equal to the index $\tau(A)$ of the restriction of the bilinear form $x y[M^{4k}]$ to $A$. There are now two cases to consider. If $n$ and $m$ are not divisible by 4 then $\tau(A) = 0$. If $n$ and $m$ are divisible by 4 then $\tau(A) = \tau(V^n) \cdot \tau(W^m)$. This completes the proof of II). A more detailed proof can be found in {\color{red} CHERN-HIRZEBRUCH-SERRE [1]}.

III) is proved by {\color{red} THOM [1]}. The proof can be summarised briefly as follows. Suppose that $V^{4k}$ is the oriented boundary of $X^{4k+1}$, and that $j: V^{4k} \to X^{4k+1}$ is the embedding. THOM considers the diagram of homomorphisms
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
H^{2k}(X^{4k+1}, \mathbf{R}) \arrow[r, "j^*"] \arrow[d] & H^{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbf{R}) \arrow[r] \arrow[d, "i"] & H^{2k+1}(X^{4k+1} \text{ mod } V^{4k}, \mathbf{R}) \arrow[d] \\
H_{2k+1}(X^{4k+1} \text{ mod } V^{4k}, \mathbf{R}) \arrow[r] & H_{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbf{R}) \arrow[r, "j_*"] & H_{2k}(X^{4k+1}, \mathbf{R})
\end{tikzcd}
\]

Here the rows are part of exact homology and cohomology sequences, and the vertical arrows are isomorphisms, defined by {\color{red} Poincaré} duality for $V^{4k}$ and $X^{4k+1}$, which make the squares commutative.

Let $A^{2k}$ be the image of $j^*$ in $H^{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbb{R})$ and let $K_{2k}$ be the kernel of $j_*$ in $H_{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbb{R})$. Then $A^{2k}$ is dual to $H_{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbb{R})/K_{2k}$ under the duality between $H^{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbb{R})$ and $H_{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbb{R})$. On the other hand, the diagram implies that, for $x \in H^{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbb{R})$,

\[
    x \in A^{2k} \Leftrightarrow i(x) \in K_{2k}.
\]

Therefore, if $b_{2k} = \dim H_{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbb{R})$ is the $2k$-th {\color{red} Betti} number of $V$,

\[
    \dim A^{2k} = \dim K_{2k} = b_{2k} - \dim K_{2k}
\]
and
\[
    \dim A^{2k} = \frac{1}{2}b_{2k}.
\]

If $x = j^* y \in A^{2k}$, and $v$ is the fundamental cycle of $V^{4k}$ then $x^2[V^{4k}] = (j^* y^2)[v] = (y^2)[j_* v] = 0$. Therefore the cone $\{x \in H^{2k}(V^{4k}, \mathbb{R}); x^2[V^{4k}] = 0\}$ contains the linear subspace $A^{2k}$ of dimension $\frac{1}{2}b_{2k}$. It follows that the bilinear form $x y[V^{4k}]$ has $p^+ = p^-$ (8.1) and hence that $\tau(V^{4k}) = 0$. This proves III) and completes the proof of Theorem 8.2.1.


\end{Proof}

\subsection{Why this definition works}

It is standard to show that all line bundles are associated to a class in $H^2(M)$: see~\cite{NateKTheory} for
the topological proof and~\cite{NateAlgGeo} for the divisor equivalent of this proof. This does not generalize
to higher dimension (there are counter-examples on $K3$ surfaces, see~\cite{hatcherVectorBundlesKTheory2017}).
Simply put, Chern classes do not hold enough information to distinguish vector bundles isomorphism classes.
Thus, the same geometric/algebraic correspondence would not work if we considered higher dimensional line
bundles. This is where the tools of \emph{intersection theory} with \emph{Chow rings} would work better, in
particular an algebraic cocycle and the operational Chern classes will be in correspondence with geometric
objects. However, even Chow rings don't remember the original intersection information, which is why these
object are still technically ``derived'' objects.

\begin{thm}{Atiyah-Serre On Vector Bundles}{atiyah-serreOnVecBundles}
  Let $X$ be either a compact complex manifold or an algebraic variety; in either case of dimension $d
  =\dim_\C X$. Let $E$ be a rank $r> d$ vector bundle on $X$ that is smooth or algebraic whether $X$ is a
  manifold of variety respectively \footnote{If algebraic, assume $\Gamma_\alg(X, \CO_X(E)) \to E_x$ is
  surjective for all $x$;i.e. it is generated by its global sections}. Then there is a trivial bundle
  $\BI^{r-d}$ of rank $r-d$  that fits into the short exact sequence:
  \[
    0 \to \BI^{r-d} \to E \to E'' \to 0
  \]
  and $\rk(E'') = d$.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  If $E$ is smooth, then by partitions of unity it is always generated by its global sections. As algebraic
  vector bundles need not be fine, we must assume they are (for example $\CO_{\P^1}(-1)$ does not satisfy this
  condition). Without loss of generality, we shall work over $\overline{k} = \C$.

  Then, for each $x \in E$, $\Gamma(X, \CO_X(E)) \twoheadrightarrow E_x$ surjective onto the fiber $E_x$. Then
  we can choose a finite dimensional subspace $W(x) \sse \Gamma(X, \CO_X(E))$  as $W(x) \to E_x$ surjective. Then
  either $\C$- or $Z$-near $x$, $\Gamma(X, \CO_X(E))$ surjects onto the fibers in the neighbourhood (namely,
  the determinant is smooth/regular, and hence we can follow a similar argument in both cases). As $X$ is
  [quasi]-compact, there exists a finite open cover with this property.

  Label these choices $W(i)$. Taking all their generators, we have a finite dimensional subspace $W \sse
  \Gamma(X, \CO_X(E))$ such that $W \twoheadrightarrow E_x$ surjects for all $ x\in X$ (namely $\sigma \mapsto
  \sigma(x)$ surjects). Let:
  \[
    \ker(x) = \ker(W \to E_x)
  \]

  Then as $\dim E_x = r$,
  \[
    \dim \ker(x) = \dim W - r
  \]
  As the dimension is constant, $\dim\ker(x)$ is independent of $X$, thus we can take their union of $X$. To
  be precise, let $\P(\ker(x))\sse \P(W)$ be the projectivizations of $\ker(x) \sse W$ (i.e. collection of all
  lines). Then consider
  \[
    Z = \cup_{x \in X} \P(\ker(x))
  \]
  and takes its $Z$-closure if necessary. Then:
  \[
    \dim Z = \dim X + \dim W - r-1 = \dim W  + d-r-1
  \]
  where the $-1$ comes from the fact that $\dim(\P(V))  = \dim V - 1$. Hence, we have:
  \[
    \codim(Z \hookrightarrow \P(W)) = r-d
  \]
  Then it is simple algebraic/affine geometry to see there exists a projective (linear) subspace $T \sse \P$ with $\dim
  T = r-d-1$ such that
  \[
    T\cap Z = \emptyset
  \]
  Let $T = \P(S)$ for some subspace $S \sse W$ (so $\dim S - r-d)$. Then by construction:
  \[
    X\times S = X \times \C^{r-d} = \BI^{r-d}
  \]
  From this, we can define a map $\BI^{r-d} \hookrightarrow E$ sending $(x,s) \in s(x) \in E$. As $T\cap
  Z  = \emptyset$, $s(x)$ is never zero, and hence $\im(\BI^{r-d} \hookrightarrow E)$ has full rank at each
  point. Letting $E'' = E/\im(\BI^{r-d} \hookrightarrow E)$, we get a vector bundle of dimension $d$
  and a short exact sequence:
  \[
    0 \to \BI^{r-d} \to E \to E'' \to 0
  \]
  as we sought to show.
\end{Proof}

Now, by section~\ref{cha:characteristicClass} we have that $c_i(\BI^r) = 0$ for all $i$, so by additivity of
characteristic classes we have that $c_1(E) = c_1(E'')$. As $c_1(E) = c_1 \left(\bigwedge^r E\right)$, we get:

  c_1(E) = c_1 \left(\bigwedge^{\rk(E'')}E''\right)

Using this, we can prove the following ``simple'' generalization:

\begin{thm}{RR For Riemann Surface With Vector Bundles}{rrForRiemannSurfaceWithVecBundles}
  Let $X$ be a compact Riemann surface and $E$ a complex vector bundle over $X$ of rank $r$. Then:
  \[
    \chi(X, E) = c_1(E) + r(1-g)
  \]
  whee $c_1(E)$ is the first Chern class of $E$.
\end{thm}

\begin{Proof}
  Start with embedding $X$ in $\P^N$ via an algebraic equation and pushforward $E$ so that it is an algebraic
  vector bundle. Then $E$ need not be generated by global holomorphic functions, however by twistign it enough
  \[
    E(n) := E\otimes \CO_X(n) \cong E\otimes \CO_X^{\otimes n}
  \]
  we shall have a very ample sheaf, and hence $E(n)$ shall have enough global section
  (see~\cite[section~4.4]{NateAlgGeo} for the details). From this, we can
  apply~\cref{th:atiyah-serreOnVecBundles}:
  \[
    0 \to \BI^{r-1} \to E(n) \to E'' \to 0
  \]
  where $\rk(E'') = 1$. Then twisting it by $\CO_X(-n)$ we get:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:RRonRiemanSurfLineBundle}
    0 \to \Dcup_{r-1} \CO_X(-n) \to E \to E''(-n) \to 0
  \end{equation}
  Then:
  \[
    \chi(X, \CO_X(E)) = \chi(X, E''(-n)) + \chi \left( \Dcup_{r-1} \CO_X(-n)\right)
  \]
  As $\rk(E''(-n)) = 1$, by ordinary Riemann-Roch we have:
  \[
    \chi(X, E''(-n)) = c_1(E''(-n)) +1-g
  \]
  giving us:
  \[
    \chi(X, \CO_X(E)) = c_1(E''(-n)) +1-g  + \chi \left( \Dcup_{r-1} \CO_X(-n)\right)
  \]
  From this, we shall proceed with inducting on the rank $r$. The base case $r = 1$
  was~\cref{th:rrForRiemannSurfacewithLineBundles}. Assume Riemann Roch holds for $r-1$, so that by the
  inductive hypothesis:
  \[
    \chi \left( \Dcup_{r-1} \CO_X(-n)\right) = c_1 \left(\Dcup_{r-1} \CO_X(-n)\right) +(r-1)(1-g)
  \]
  Then:
  \[
    \chi(X, \CO_X(E)) = c_1(E''(-n)) +1-g  +c_1 \left(\Dcup_{r-1} \CO_X(-n)\right) +(r-1)(1-g)
  \]
  Then we can combine the Chern classes by additivity on equation~(\ref{eq:RRonRiemanSurfLineBundle}), and so;
  \[
    \chi(X, \CO_X(E)) = c_1(E) + r(1-g)
  \]
  as we sought to show.
\end{Proof}
